<!-- section: ESS-THEMES -->
# Two cross-cutting patterns explain most of the control exposure and determine the priorities for action
## Several functions do not hold answers about key controls and this uncertainty limits confident decision making and sequencing
A set of outstanding questions was answered "not known" by accountable functions, which means leadership cannot assume the presence or operation of important reporting, verification and response processes without first settling those answers; unresolved ownership of these questions prevents accurate prioritisation of work. <!-- refs: THEME-UNCERTAINTY, N-OUTSTANDING -->

When functions cannot say whether a control exists or operates, the practical consequence is that prioritisation and resource decisions are made with partial information, which risks slow action, duplicated effort or misdirected investment; resolving these unknowns is therefore a necessary precursor to efficient control design and operation. <!-- refs: THEME-UNCERTAINTY, N-OUTSTANDING-FUNCTIONS -->

The leadership implication is to assign owners for every outstanding question, require accountable functions to provide definitive answers within a short window, and use those settled answers to create a clear order of work so that design and operating efforts address the most consequential gaps first. <!-- refs: THEME-UNCERTAINTY, N-OUTSTANDING -->

## Verification steps that materially change payment routing or identity are accepted on the requester's word across several functions and must be closed
The organisation's answers show that changes to bank details, new beneficiary payments, customer account updates and help desk resets are often verified through channels the requester controls or by information supplied in the request, which creates straightforward impersonation routes where an attacker who controls a mailbox or supplies a phone number can direct funds or access. <!-- refs: THEME-VERIFY, THEME-VERIFY-AREAS -->

Because these verification failures are present across payroll, customer management, finance and IT support, attackers have multiple, repeatable entry points and the opportunity to chain actions together, for example by compromising an account, redirecting salary payments and then using that access to alter beneficiary details or approve refunds. <!-- refs: THEME-VERIFY, THEME-VERIFY-N -->

Leadership should therefore adopt a cross-area standard: any confirmation that materially changes payment routing or identity must be initiated by the organisation through a route the requester does not control, such as a call-back to a number on file, manager confirmation through a known channel, or an in-person check, and these requirements should be applied consistently across affected functions. <!-- refs: THEME-VERIFY, THEME-VERIFY-NAREAS -->

<!-- section: ESS-FINDINGS -->
# The following findings were selected from the scenarios and answers to show where controls should be designed and run first
The selection rule for these findings is: resolve scenarios with answers to settle or questions to clarify first, then select one scenario from each theme in turn prioritising those with the most core controls reported absent and the most related practices reported weaker; this selection method is descriptive and is not an approved ranking. <!-- refs: N-FINDINGS -->

## Salaries can be redirected through a fake bank change when confirmations rely on the requester's channel
[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-01]]
The organisation reports that changes to an employee's bank details are confirmed by replying to the email or form that requested the change, which means the control to confirm bank changes by using a route the organisation initiates is only partly in place. <!-- refs: FINDING-01 -->

When confirmation is completed on the same route the requester uses, an attacker who can send a convincing email, forge a form or compromise an inbox can request a banking change and intercept or respond to the confirmation, causing payroll to redirect salary payments to an account the attacker controls. <!-- refs: FINDING-01, AREA-D5-GAP -->

Good practice is to require an organisation-initiated confirmation for every bank detail change, for example a call to a phone number already on file or an in-person check, and to require a logged, auditable record of the confirmation before payroll systems are updated, so that changes cannot be completed solely on the basis of an incoming request. <!-- refs: FINDING-01, PRIORITY-01 -->

The immediate management action is to mandate the priority control that requires organisation-initiated confirmation, to produce a documented workflow and training for HR and payroll staff, and to begin operating the control so that every bank change is recorded and the HR and payroll lead operates the process under the Chief financial officer's accountability. <!-- refs: FINDING-01, PRIORITY-01 -->

## A false identity can enter at hiring when identity documents are copied but not verified against authoritative sources
[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-02]]
The organisation reports that a copy of a new employee's identity document is taken at onboarding but that the document is not verified against Home Affairs or a recognised verification service, leaving the identity verification control only partly present. <!-- refs: FINDING-02 -->

Without an authoritative verification step, a person can be employed under a stolen or fictitious identity or with false qualifications, which creates the risks of ghost or fictitious employees appearing on payroll, and of inappropriate access to systems or sites granted to a person who is not who they claim to be. <!-- refs: FINDING-02, AREA-D5-GAP -->

Good practice is to verify each new hire's identity against a reliable, authoritative source before the employment start date and before payroll or system access is enabled, to retain the verification result in the personnel record, and to make verification a gating step in onboarding so that activation cannot proceed on the basis of an unchecked copy. <!-- refs: FINDING-02, PRIORITY-02 -->

The first step is to select and contract with a verification service or to adopt an established government-linked verification route, update onboarding procedures to make verification mandatory before payroll activation, and assign the HR and payroll lead to operate the verification with the Chief financial officer accountable for compliance. <!-- refs: FINDING-02, PRIORITY-02 -->

## A new beneficiary payment can be released on a fake instruction when confirmations use contact details supplied in the request
[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-03]]
The organisation reports that requests to pay a new beneficiary are confirmed either by replying to the email or by calling a number given in the request, which means the control to confirm new beneficiaries by using contact details already on record is only partly in place. <!-- refs: FINDING-03 -->

When finance confirmations rely on contact details supplied in the payment request, an attacker who controls a supplier mailbox or who poses as a supplier can give a number they control and receive the callback confirmation, resulting in payments being released to fraudulent accounts that are controlled by the attacker. <!-- refs: FINDING-03, AREA-D6-GAP -->

Good practice is to require that every payment to a new beneficiary be confirmed by calling the requester on a number already held in the organisation's records, or through an independently documented relationship, and to log that confirmation before any payment is released so there is an auditable trail preventing acceptance of contact information from the request itself. <!-- refs: FINDING-03, PRIORITY-03 -->

To act immediately, finance should adopt a templated confirmation process, maintain a secure register of authorised contact numbers, train payment staff to follow the template and record confirmations, and the Finance lead should operate the control while the Chief financial officer remains accountable for its adoption and reporting. <!-- refs: FINDING-03, PRIORITY-03 -->

## Customer details can be changed to divert money or goods when changes are only checked if they appear unusual
[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-04]]
The organisation reports that changes to customer records such as refund accounts, delivery addresses or contact numbers are checked only if they look unusual, which means routine changes are often accepted without independent verification and the core control to verify customer detail changes is only partly present. <!-- refs: FINDING-04 -->

When the default is to accept ordinary-looking requests without independent confirmation, an attacker or an insider can alter refund accounts or delivery details and then request refunds or deliveries to addresses they control, which may be difficult to detect if reconciliations rely on the altered customer record or if the change is paired with fraudulently authorised credit notes. <!-- refs: FINDING-04, AREA-D7-GAP -->

Good practice is to verify any change to a customer's refund account, delivery address or contact number with the customer using details already on file, to ensure changes are executed by someone outside sales, and to retain the verification record so that a later reconciliation or investigation can trace the change to an authenticated confirmation. <!-- refs: FINDING-04, PRIORITY-04 -->

Management's first action should be to require independent verification of customer detail changes, to place execution responsibility outside the sales function, to design a script for verification, and to measure compliance so the Commercial and customer lead can operate the control under the Chief financial officer's accountability. <!-- refs: FINDING-04, PRIORITY-04 -->

## A help desk reset can hand an attacker access when call-backs are made to numbers the caller supplies
[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-05]]
The organisation reports that the help desk calls the person back when a password or multi-factor reset is requested, but that the call-back is made to a number the caller supplies, which means the control to verify resets through a route the organisation initiates is only partly present. <!-- refs: FINDING-05 -->

If the help desk calls a number provided by the caller, an impersonator can supply a number they control and receive the call-back, enabling a password or multi-factor reset that gives the attacker access to the employee's account; once inside, the attacker can use legitimate account privileges to change bank details or approve payments, making activity look like normal user behaviour. <!-- refs: FINDING-05, AREA-D9-GAP -->

Good practice is to require that password and multi-factor resets be verified via a route the caller cannot control, for example a call-back to a number already on file, manager confirmation through a separate channel, or a documented secondary verification step, and to log every reset decision so that resets are auditable. <!-- refs: FINDING-05, PRIORITY-05 -->

The immediate step is to update help desk procedures so that call-backs use a number on file or a manager confirmation through a known channel, to train help desk staff on the revised process, to enforce logging of every reset, and to have the IT lead operate the change with the Chief financial officer accountable for reporting on its first cycles. <!-- refs: FINDING-05, PRIORITY-05 -->

<!-- section: ESS-PATHWAYS -->
# The help desk reset pathway shows how an account compromise can be turned into a payment or identity change
## An impersonator can get a reset through a help desk call-back route that the caller controls and then use the account to move money or change beneficiary details
The pathway begins when an outsider impersonates an employee to the help desk or to an external IT provider and requests a password or multi-factor reset while claiming a lost device or urgency; the reset succeeds when the help desk confirms the request using contact details the caller provides rather than a route the organisation initiates. <!-- refs: PATHWAY-01, FINDING-05 -->

Once the attacker has access to the employee's account they can access systems that change bank details or approve payments and can complete actions that move money or alter beneficiary details; because the actions originate from a legitimate account under legitimate credentials, they may look like ordinary work and can be hard to detect without specific monitoring for anomalous resets followed by payment activity. <!-- refs: PATHWAY-01, FINDING-05 -->

Warning indicators include an urgent reset request or a claimed lost device, a reset followed shortly by payment or bank-detail activity, sign-ins from unusual locations or devices, or an employee being unaware that a reset was initiated in their name; containment requires immediate account lock, reversal of unauthorised changes where feasible, notification and liaison with banks or counterparties, and preservation of help desk and sign-in records for investigation. <!-- refs: PATHWAY-01, FINDING-05 -->

<!-- section: ESS-PRIORITIES -->
# The immediate priority controls are the verification steps that stop quick impersonation routes and the route that ensures allegations against senior people are received independently
[[exhibit:priorities]]
The priorities set out below are the first responses to design, approve and start operating so that the most direct routes for money and identity change are closed and outstanding questions are assigned owners and settled. Each priority identifies why it matters, what to establish, who is accountable and who operates it. <!-- refs: PRIORITY-01, PRIORITY-02 -->

## Bank changes must be confirmed with the employee through a route the organisation starts
Every change to an employee's bank details must be confirmed by a channel the organisation initiates, such as a call to a number on file or an in-person verification, and the confirmation must be recorded before payroll updates are made to prevent changes that are initiated and confirmed on the same requester-controlled channel. <!-- refs: PRIORITY-01 -->

To establish this control HR and payroll should produce a documented confirmation workflow, train payroll staff on the approved script and the logging requirements, and implement a simple audit trail so each bank change is associated with a recorded confirmation before the payroll system is updated; practical ownership sits with the HR and payroll lead while the Chief financial officer is accountable. <!-- refs: PRIORITY-01 -->

## Identity verification must be part of every onboarding process before payroll or system access is enabled
Every new employee should have their identity verified against Home Affairs records or a recognised verification service before the employment start date and before payroll activation or system access is granted, with the verification result retained in personnel records to provide an auditable gate in onboarding. <!-- refs: PRIORITY-02 -->

Operational steps include selecting and contracting a verification service, integrating verification into the HR system or onboarding workflow, updating job acceptance letters to make verification a precondition of activation, and training HR staff to retain and record verification results; the HR and payroll lead should operate the service under the Chief financial officer's accountability. <!-- refs: PRIORITY-02 -->

## New beneficiary payments must be confirmed by calling a number already on record before release
Every payment instruction to a new beneficiary must be confirmed by calling the requester on a number that is already held in the organisation's secure records or by using a separately documented relationship method, and the confirmation must be logged before any payment is released so that contact information provided in the payment request cannot be trusted on its own. <!-- refs: PRIORITY-03 -->

Implementing this control requires finance to adopt a templated confirmation process, to maintain a secure register of authorised contact numbers and to require payment staff to record confirmations against the payment request; the Finance lead should run the operation and the Chief financial officer should report on compliance. <!-- refs: PRIORITY-03 -->

## Customer detail changes must be verified independently and executed outside sales
Changes to a customer's refund bank account, delivery address or contact numbers must be verified with the customer through details already on file and must be executed by someone outside the sales team, with a recorded confirmation to create an auditable change history and to reduce the risk of diversion through routine customer updates. <!-- refs: PRIORITY-04 -->

Establishing this control means designing a verification script, placing execution responsibility with a separate customer operations function or a designated administrator, training staff and requiring logged evidence before the customer record is updated, with the Commercial and customer lead operating the control and the Chief financial officer accountable for adoption and oversight. <!-- refs: PRIORITY-04 -->

## Help desk resets must be verified through a route the requester does not control and must be logged
Every password or multi-factor reset request must be verified through a route the caller cannot control, such as a call-back to a number on file, manager confirmation through a separate known channel, or another documented secondary verification, and every reset must be logged to provide an audit trail for later investigation. <!-- refs: PRIORITY-05 -->

Practical rollout requires the IT lead to revise help desk procedures, to update the service desk knowledge base and scripts, to train staff in the requirement to use stored contact details or documented manager confirmations, and to implement mandatory logging of reset requests and confirmations, with the Chief financial officer accountable for monitoring the first operating cycles. <!-- refs: PRIORITY-05 -->

## A written route must take allegations about senior people to an independent recipient and be communicated to staff
There should be a documented route that takes allegations concerning any executive or board member to an independent recipient and that is communicated across the organisation so employees know how to raise concerns about senior people without undue fear of reprisal or suppression. <!-- refs: PRIORITY-06 -->

To implement this route the board or its audit or risk committee should design the intake path and nominate an independent recipient, the Chief executive should ensure the route is documented and communicated, and the Board or audit committee should oversee that the route is available, understood and used when necessary. <!-- refs: PRIORITY-06 -->

# The proposed thirty, sixty and ninety day stages describe what should be true as priorities are accepted and run
## The organisation should use the first stage to establish facts, name owners and decide the order of work
In the proposed [[STAGE-30]] window the immediate objectives are to assign owners to every outstanding question, to collect missing answers from accountable functions, and to decide the order of work so that design efforts address the most consequential control gaps first. <!-- refs: STAGE-30 -->

Expected outcomes for this stage include every outstanding question answered or assigned to a named owner, each open area with a named accountable executive, and a recorded order of work with factors and dependencies documented; practical owners for this stage include legal and compliance, fraud risk, HR, payroll, finance and IT leads working under executive direction. <!-- refs: STAGE-30 -->

## The organisation should use the second stage to approve designs and schedule the first operating cycles
In the proposed [[STAGE-60]] window the work is to approve the design, owner and record for each priority control, to finalise operating procedures and to schedule the first operating cycle so each control can be observed and adjusted in live operation. <!-- refs: STAGE-60 -->

Outcomes for this stage should include design approvals for each priority control, owners assigned for day-to-day operation, completed training materials and an agreed schedule for the first operating cycles, with the practical owners preparing the first operation and the oversight body ready to review progress. <!-- refs: STAGE-60 -->

## The organisation should use the third stage to operate each priority and report status to the oversight body
In the proposed [[STAGE-90]] window each priority control should have run at least once and have recorded evidence of operation, and status should be reported to the board or its audit or risk committee so leadership can see the controls operating and decide on next steps. <!-- refs: STAGE-90 -->

Outcomes include running records for each priority, evidence that confirmations and verifications have been recorded and that outstanding questions have been settled or assigned to longer-term work, and an initial performance report to the oversight body prepared by the accountable executives. <!-- refs: STAGE-90 -->

<!-- section: ESS-CONCLUSION -->
# Leadership should settle the outstanding questions and start the verification controls that close direct impersonation routes within the proposed programme
Leaders should recognise that settling the outstanding questions held by several functions is a necessary precursor to efficient design work, and that implementing a small set of verification controls will materially reduce the most direct routes that impersonators use to move money or change identity. <!-- refs: THEME-UNCERTAINTY, THEME-VERIFY -->

Within the proposed [[STAGE-90]] programme the organisation should aim to have named owners for outstanding questions, approved designs for the priority verification controls and operational evidence that each priority has run at least once so the board or its audit or risk committee can receive an initial operating report and decide the subsequent programme. The sensible immediate step is to name owners for outstanding questions and to mandate the design of the priority verification controls so that work in the second stage can proceed without delay. <!-- refs: STAGE-90, PRIORITY-01, PRIORITY-02 -->
