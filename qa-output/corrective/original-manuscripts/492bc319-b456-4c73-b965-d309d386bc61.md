# Management-level patterns explain the reported position and require leadership decisions
## Several functions do not hold answers about their own controls, so uncertainty limits assurance
Many functions answered questions with “not known”, which means the organisation cannot currently demonstrate ownership of important preventative and detective practices.
<!-- refs: THEME-UNCERTAINTY, N-OUTSTANDING-FUNCTIONS -->
When functional owners cannot describe whether they operate key steps such as suspicious transaction reporting, compromise playbooks or verification for voice and video, the organisation is exposed because responsibility for routine decisions and incident response is unclear. Leadership should expect to allocate clear owners and to gather basic evidence promptly so the oversight body can judge whether answers reflect an absence of controls or a lack of documentation.
<!-- refs: THEME-UNCERTAINTY, N-OUTSTANDING -->
## Instructions that move money or change identity are accepted on the requester's word and create cross-area vulnerability
The organisation’s answers show recurring weaknesses in how changes to bank details, beneficiary instructions, customer information and help desk resets are authorised and confirmed, which together allow impersonation or coerced changes to translate quickly into payments or account takeovers.
<!-- refs: THEME-VERIFY, THEME-VERIFY-AREAS -->
Because these weaknesses cross people, commercial, IT and finance functions, a set of simple verification rules that the organisation enforces consistently will materially reduce the likelihood that an impersonator converts a request into a payment or account control change. Leadership should decide a small set of verification routes that nobody outside the organisation can control and require them for all requests that move money or change identity.

<!-- section: ESS-FINDINGS -->
# [[N-FINDINGS|w]] findings show where controls are missing in ways an impersonator could exploit
Selection rule: findings were chosen from scenarios with questions to settle and from the verification theme to show representative gaps and their immediate remedies.
<!-- refs: N-FINDINGS, THEME-VERIFY -->
## Salaries can be redirected through a fake bank change
The organisation reports that bank changes are confirmed by replying to the email or form that requested them, which means confirmations flow through a route the requester controls rather than a route the organisation starts.
<!-- refs: FINDING-01, THEME-VERIFY -->
When confirmation follows the same channel an impersonator controls, an attacker who can compromise an inbox or manufacture convincing emails and forms can alter payroll instructions and receive redirected salaries. This mode of fraud is fast and can spread before detection unless confirmation is routed independently.
<!-- refs: FINDING-01, THEME-VERIFY -->
Good practice is that every change to payee details is confirmed through a route the organisation starts, such as a call to a number already on file, a face to face check or a secure portal requiring the employee to authenticate by a method not supplied in the change request. This operating rule reduces the chance that an attacker converts an email or form into a payment direction.
<!-- refs: FINDING-01, THEME-VERIFY -->
The first action is to make bank-change confirmation a formal control owned by HR and payroll with a clear record: every change should be subject to a call-back or in-person check before it takes effect, and the HR and payroll lead should report implementation to the accountable executive within the proposed stage. This control can be implemented quickly and will directly address the salary redirection exposure.
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-01, FINDING-01 -->
[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-01]]
## A false identity can enter at hiring because identity documents are not verified
The organisation records that a copy of the identity document is taken at hiring but that it is not checked against a reliable source, which leaves recruitment open to invented or stolen identities.
<!-- refs: FINDING-02, THEME-VERIFY -->
If identity checks rely only on a paper copy or a document image without verification against an authoritative source, someone can be employed under a false identity and later use their position to create fictitious suppliers, approve payments or otherwise exploit insider access. This exposure is hard to unwind after the fact.
<!-- refs: FINDING-02, THEME-VERIFY -->
Good practice is to verify every new employee’s identity against Home Affairs records or an equivalent verification service before they start, and to record the result in the HR file so hires are demonstrably checked. Verifying identity at intake makes it much harder for ghost employees and impersonators to gain a foothold.
<!-- refs: FINDING-02, PRIORITY-02 -->
The first step is to require identity verification as a hiring gate, owned by HR with results filed before payroll or system access is provisioned; the HR and payroll lead should present a schedule of completed verifications to the accountable executive within the proposed stage. This step reduces the risk once implemented.
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-02, FINDING-02 -->
[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-02]]
## A new beneficiary can be paid on a fake instruction when confirmations use caller-supplied contacts
The organisation reports that new beneficiary requests are confirmed by replying to the email or calling the number in the request, so confirmations are usually made through the route the requester controls.
<!-- refs: FINDING-03, THEME-VERIFY -->
When confirmations are made to contact details supplied by the requester, an impersonator who submits a fraudulent invoice with a controlled phone number or email can cause an authorised payment to be sent to a fraudulent account, including by impersonating senior authority for urgent transfers. This is a common mechanism for supplier payment fraud.
<!-- refs: FINDING-03, THEME-VERIFY -->
Good practice requires that before any payment to a new beneficiary is executed someone in finance calls a number already on record for the requester or uses another independent route to verify the instruction, and that the call-back is logged in the payment file. This process substantially reduces the chance that an invoice from an impersonator is paid.
<!-- refs: FINDING-03, PRIORITY-03 -->
The immediate action is to formalise a call-back rule for new beneficiaries, assign practical ownership to finance, and require evidence of the independent confirmation before payments are released; the finance lead should implement the rule and report its first runs in the proposed stage.
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-03, FINDING-03 -->
[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-03]]
## Customer details can be changed to divert money or goods because verification is inconsistent
The organisation indicates customer detail changes are validated only when they look unusual, which leaves standardised or modest edits unchecked and therefore exploitable.
<!-- refs: FINDING-04, THEME-VERIFY -->
If customer refunds, delivery addresses or contact numbers can be altered without verification with the customer and without separation from the sales function, attackers and dishonest insiders can divert refunds and goods or manipulate revenue records. This mechanism enables both external fraud and internal collusion.
<!-- refs: FINDING-04, THEME-VERIFY -->
A practical control is that all material customer-detail changes are verified through a contact already on file and performed by an individual outside sales, with a short audit trail kept for each change. This operating rule reduces the opportunities for deception and helps detect patterns.
<!-- refs: FINDING-04, PRIORITY-04 -->
Management should require that the commercial and customer lead implements a verification workflow that records the verification route and separates the authority to change details from sales, then report the first set of changes and the verification records to the accountable executive within the proposed stage.
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-04, FINDING-04 -->
[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-04]]
## A help desk reset can hand an attacker access because call-backs use caller-supplied numbers
The organisation reports that help desk resets are verified by calling back the requester on a number the caller gives, which means the help desk may use contact information an impersonator supplies rather than a number the organisation holds.
<!-- refs: FINDING-05, THEME-VERIFY -->
When help desk processes accept a reset on the strength of how convincing a caller sounds or on a number supplied in the call, an attacker who social engineers the help desk can gain control of accounts and then use legitimate access to alter bank details, approve payments or exfiltrate data. This is a typical route to account takeover.
<!-- refs: FINDING-05, THEME-VERIFY -->
Good practice requires that every password or multi-factor reset is verified through a route the caller does not control, such as a call-back to a number already on file, manager confirmation through a separate channel, or a secure ticketing system that ties reset authority to recorded credentials. This change reduces social engineering risk and preserves an audit trail.
<!-- refs: FINDING-05, PRIORITY-05 -->
The first management action is for the IT lead to change reset procedures so call-backs use numbers already on file or other independent verification, to keep a record of every reset and to brief the help desk on the new rule within the proposed stage, with the accountable executive reviewing progress.
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-05, FINDING-05 -->

<!-- section: ESS-PATHWAYS -->
# A plausible pathway shows how a help desk reset hands an attacker the account that moves money
The pathway begins with an outsider impersonating an employee to the help desk, claiming a lost device or inability to sign in, and therefore requesting a password or multi-factor reset.
<!-- refs: PATHWAY-01, FINDING-05 -->
Because resets are validated on the caller’s word and call-backs are made to numbers the caller supplies, the help desk carries out the reset; the attacker signs in and uses legitimate access to change bank details or to approve payments in systems that accept that identity. The weakness relied on is the absence of verification through a route the caller does not control and the lack of strong sign-in protection on payment systems.
<!-- refs: PATHWAY-01, THEME-VERIFY -->
Warning indicators include an urgent reset claim, a reset followed quickly by unusual payment activity, sign-ins from new locations or devices, and an employee who says they did not request a reset; containment requires locking the account, reversing changes where possible, preserving sign-in and help desk logs and notifying the bank and affected parties promptly. The long-term change is to require independent call-back routes and strong sign-in protections for systems that can move money.

<!-- section: ESS-PRIORITIES -->
# Management should implement [[N-PRIORITIES|w]] first responses that settle questions and close the verification gaps
## Bank changes must be confirmed with the employee through a route the organisation starts
Every change to an employee’s bank details should be confirmed by a call-back or in-person check to a contact the organisation already holds before the change is applied, eliminating reliance on the requester’s channel. This control directly addresses salary redirection and is simple to operate.
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-01, FINDING-01 -->
The accountable executive is the chief financial officer and the practical owner is the HR and payroll lead; the proposal sets the change to be designed and scheduled in the proposed stage so the board or its committee can receive an early report on implementation.
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-01, STAGE-60 -->
## Identity verification must be performed for every new employee before they start
Require every new employee’s identity to be verified against Home Affairs records or an equivalent service and retain the verification result before payroll or access provisioning proceeds, preventing phantom or fictitious hires. This measure reduces insider and ghost-employee risks.
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-02, FINDING-02 -->
The chief financial officer is the accountable executive and the HR and payroll lead is the practical owner; the control can be specified and scheduled in the proposed stage and its initial runs reported to the oversight body.
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-02, STAGE-60 -->
## New beneficiaries must be confirmed by call-back to a number already on record
Before releasing a payment to any new beneficiary, finance should call a number already on file for the requester and log the confirmation in the payment record, so an invoice or payment request cannot be validated against contact details supplied by the requester. This is a preventative control aimed at invoice and supplier fraud.
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-03, FINDING-03 -->
The chief financial officer is accountable and the finance lead is the practical owner; the proposed stage sets design and test runs so the board receives evidence the rule is operating.
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-03, STAGE-60 -->
## Customer detail changes must be verified with the customer and moved out of sales
All material changes to a customer’s refund account, delivery address or contact number should be verified through a contact already on file and executed by someone independent of sales, with a short audit trail kept for each change to detect patterns. This reduces risks of diverted goods, false refunds and manipulated receivables.
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-04, FINDING-04 -->
The chief financial officer is accountable and the commercial and customer lead is the practical owner; the control is designed in the proposed stage and its records are reported to the oversight body as part of routine updates.
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-04, STAGE-60 -->
## Help desk resets must be verified through a route the caller does not control
Reset requests should be verified by a call-back to a number already on file, a manager confirmation through a separate known channel, or a secure ticketing workflow, and every reset should be recorded, reducing social engineering success against account credentials.
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-05, FINDING-05 -->
The chief financial officer is the accountable executive and the IT lead is the practical owner; the proposed stage schedules the design and briefing of help desk staff and requires the first run reports to be provided to the oversight body.
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-05, STAGE-60 -->
## A route must exist to take allegations about senior people to an independent recipient
Create and communicate a written route that ensures allegations about executives or board members reach an independent recipient, so concerns about senior people are handled outside ordinary reporting lines and do not go unchallenged. This control addresses the risk that seniority creates exceptions.
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-06, L-AREAS-OPEN -->
The chief executive is accountable and the board or audit committee is the practical owner; the action should be specified in the proposed stage and its existence communicated to staff with evidence provided to the oversight body.
