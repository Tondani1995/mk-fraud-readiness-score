# Two management-level patterns explain why verification controls that matter are unreliable and why oversight must act
## Several functions do not hold answers about their own controls and that uncertainty reduces confidence in detection and response
Multiple functions answered "not known" when asked about procedures and records the organisation needs to detect and respond to fraud, leaving unresolved questions about suspicious transaction reporting, response decisions, declared interests, exceptions for seniority, recall procedures, human review before blocking customers, loss tracking, verification for non-email channels, compromise playbooks, notification under privacy law, speak-up arrangements and a single allegations intake. 
<!-- refs: THEME-UNCERTAINTY, N-OUTSTANDING -->
Where these function-level answers are absent, leaders cannot reliably say whether an event would trigger escalation or whether losses are being measured and analysed, which weakens oversight and delays corrective action; the leadership implication is to settle those outstanding questions promptly and name accountable owners so subsequent control design rests on firm facts. 
<!-- refs: THEME-UNCERTAINTY, N-OUTSTANDING-FUNCTIONS -->
## Instructions that move money or change identity are accepted on the requester's word across several areas and that creates multiple operational routes to loss
The organisation reports related practices weaker where instructions change a bank account, a beneficiary, customer contact details, or an identity at hiring, and the help desk confirms resets using numbers supplied by the caller, which together create multiple routes an attacker could use to divert funds or hijack accounts. 
<!-- refs: THEME-VERIFY, THEME-VERIFY-AREAS -->
Because these verification weaknesses span payroll, customer management, finance and IT, the implication for leadership is to require a common verification standard across functions and monitor adherence centrally so that one control failure does not enable an exploit across the organisation.

<!-- section: ESS-FINDINGS -->
# The [[N-FINDINGS]] findings describe operational weaknesses to be closed with the priority responses stated later
The selection rule for findings used scenarios with outstanding answers first, then one scenario from each management-level pattern choosing those with the most absent core controls; the findings below reflect the organisation's answers and are not the result of testing or external validation. 
<!-- refs: N-FINDINGS, N-OUTSTANDING-SCHEMES -->
## Salaries can be redirected through a fake bank change because changes are confirmed by reply to the request
The organisation reports that bank changes are confirmed by replying to the email or form that requested them, which means confirmation follows a route an impersonator can control, and the HR and payroll lead is named as owner in the answers. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-01 -->
If confirmation is only by reply, an attacker who can impersonate an employee by email or compromise an inbox can request a change and then confirm it through the same channel, causing payroll to pay into an account under the attacker's control; payroll processing cycles increase the chance that late changes will be actioned before review. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-01, AREA-D5-GAP -->
Good practice is to require confirmation through a channel the organisation starts, such as a call to a number already on file or in-person verification, with clear logs of the confirming contact and documented exceptions that require separate sign-off; this reduces the attacker’s ability to control both the request and the confirmation. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-01, FINDING-01 -->

[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-01]]

## A false identity can enter at hiring because identity documents are taken but not checked
The organisation reports that identity verification at hiring consists of taking a copy of the identity document without checking it against Home Affairs or a verification service, and the HR and payroll lead is noted as the owner of the practice. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-02 -->
When identity evidence is not verified with a source an impersonator cannot control, the organisation is at risk of hiring a person under a stolen or invented identity, creating ghost employees or fictitious payees that are difficult to detect later; automated provisioning on start dates can give immediate access and payment before issues are noticed. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-02, AREA-D5-GAP -->
Good practice is to verify each new employee's identity against Home Affairs or a recognised verification service before onboarding, to retain the verification result in personnel records, and to link verification outcomes to payroll and access provisioning so activation is conditional on verification. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-02, FINDING-02 -->

[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-02]]

## A new beneficiary can be paid on a fake instruction because confirmations use details supplied by the requester
The organisation reports that requests for payment to new beneficiaries are confirmed by replying to the email or calling the number in the request, and the finance lead is named as owner for the practice. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-03 -->
If confirmations rely on contact details supplied in the payment request, an impersonator who can send a convincing invoice or payment instruction can provide contact details that route the confirmation back to them, and a payment will be released to an attacker-controlled account. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-03, AREA-D6-GAP -->
Good practice is to require call-back confirmation using contact details already on record for the requester or independent confirmation through an authorised approver, and to keep an auditable entry of the confirming contact and number used. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-03, FINDING-03 -->

[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-03]]

## Customer details can be changed to divert money or goods because changes are only checked when they look unusual
The organisation reports that changes to customer refund accounts, delivery addresses and contact numbers are verified only when they look unusual, and the commercial and customer lead is recorded as owner of the practice. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-04 -->
An attacker who can impersonate a customer or compromise a customer-facing inbox can submit a routine-looking change and have refunds or deliveries redirected without the customer being contacted on details already on file; routine handling of most changes increases the chance a fraudulent change will be processed before detection. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-04, AREA-D7-GAP -->
Good practice is to verify every change to critical customer details through a route established by the organisation, to require a person outside sales to authorise changes to refund accounts, and to maintain an auditable history of changes for trend analysis. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-04, FINDING-04 -->

[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-04]]

## A help desk reset can hand an attacker access because call-backs use numbers the caller provides
The organisation reports that the help desk calls the person back on a number supplied by the caller, which means the confirmation route can be controlled by an impersonator, and the IT lead is listed as owner for the practice. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-05 -->
When resets are verified using caller-supplied numbers, an attacker who controls a phone number or can spoof caller details can obtain password or multi-factor resets and then use the account to change payment details or to authorise transactions; activity will appear under a legitimate user's credentials and so be harder to detect. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-05, AREA-D9-GAP -->
Good practice is to verify resets through a route an impersonator would not control, such as a call-back to a number on file or manager confirmation via an established channel, to log the reset and its confirmation, and to limit high-risk actions until additional checks are completed. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-05, FINDING-05 -->

<!-- section: ESS-PATHWAYS -->
# A help desk reset pathway shows how an account takeover can lead to diverted payments and why containment must be prompt
## The help desk reset pathway explains how an attacker uses a reset to move money from the organisation
An outsider impersonates an employee to the help desk and requests a password or multi-factor reset, often claiming a lost phone, and the help desk confirms the reset using a number the caller provides, giving the attacker account access. 
<!-- refs: PATHWAY-01 -->
Once the account is reset, the attacker signs in as the employee, uses legitimate access to change bank details or to submit payment requests, and because changes and payments are executed under the account's normal privileges the activity looks like ordinary work and can be concealed among genuine transactions. 
<!-- refs: PATHWAY-01 -->
Warning indicators include a reset request claiming urgency or a lost phone, a reset followed soon after by a payment or bank-detail change, sign-ins from an unusual location or device, or an employee unaware of activity in their name; containment requires locking the account, reversing unauthorised changes, contacting the bank about payments, and preserving help desk and sign-in logs for investigation. 
<!-- refs: PATHWAY-01 -->

<!-- section: ESS-PRIORITIES -->
# The [[N-PRIORITIES]] priority controls will close the routes the findings describe and settle outstanding verification questions
Each priority is a control the organisation can design and start operating quickly; they are intended to be owned by named accountable executives and practical owners and to be scheduled in the proposed stages so the oversight body can track progress. 
<!-- refs: N-PRIORITIES, N-PRIORITIES-CONTROL -->
## Bank changes must be confirmed through a channel the organisation starts to stop payroll diversion
Every change to an employee's bank details should be confirmed through a route the organisation starts, such as a call to a number on file or an in-person confirmation, and exceptions should be recorded and approved by a second person; the chief financial officer is accountable and the HR and payroll lead is the practical owner. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-01 -->
Early deliverables include a short script for the confirming contact, a mandatory confirmation field in the payroll change record, and a short review of recent changes to validate the operating practice as it starts. This control both prevents direct loss and settles an outstanding verification question about payroll. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-01, STAGE-30 -->
## Every new employee's identity must be verified against Home Affairs before onboarding to prevent ghost entrants
Identity verification should be performed against Home Affairs or an equivalent verification service before a new hire is activated in payroll and systems, with the chief financial officer accountable and the HR and payroll lead responsible for operation. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-02 -->
Operational steps include selecting a verification provider, adding a mandatory verification checkpoint to onboarding, recording verification outcomes in personnel files, and preventing payroll and systems provisioning until verification completes; these actions close the pathway a fictitious employee would use to get paid. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-02, STAGE-60 -->
## New beneficiaries must be confirmed by calling a number already on record to prevent invoice diversion
Every payment to a new beneficiary should be confirmed by calling the requester on a number already on record before release, with the chief financial officer accountable and the finance lead as practical owner, and the organisation must keep an auditable record of the confirming contact and number. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-03 -->
The immediate work is to create a finance confirmation script, to add the confirmation field to payment records, and to train the team to reject confirmations that use contact details supplied in the request; recording exceptions provides feedback on control effectiveness. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-03, STAGE-60 -->
## Every change to critical customer details must be verified with the customer through details already on file and made outside sales to prevent diversion
Changes to refund accounts, delivery addresses and contact numbers should be verified with details already on file and implemented by a person outside sales, with the chief financial officer accountable and the commercial and customer lead the practical owner. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-04 -->
Practical steps include defining which customer changes require out-of-sales verification, implementing a recorded confirmation step, and running a short audit of recent changes to identify anomalies while the control is embedded. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-04, STAGE-60 -->
## Help desk resets must be verified through a route the organisation starts to stop account takeover
Every password or multi-factor reset should be verified through a route an impersonator would not control, such as a call-back to a number on file or confirmation from a manager through an established channel, with the chief financial officer accountable and the IT lead as practical owner. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-05 -->
Implementing this control requires a revised help desk script, a record field for the confirming contact, and temporary limits on high-risk actions until additional checks are completed; early measurement is whether resets are confirmed through the organisation-started route or are an approved exception. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-05, STAGE-60 -->
## There must be a written route for allegations against senior people so concerns are escalated independently
A written route should take allegations about any executive or board member to an independent recipient and be known by employees, with the chief executive accountable and the board or its audit committee as practical owner, so concerns about senior people are not handled only inside lines they may influence. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-06 -->
The organisation should publish the route, train intake staff, and ensure the intake recipient reports on the number and status of allegations to the oversight body; making the route visible deters misconduct and reassures staff that concerns about senior people will be accepted outside the normal reporting line. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-06, STAGE-30 -->

<!-- section: ESS-ROADMAP -->
# A proposed [[STAGE-30|w]], [[STAGE-60|w]] and [[STAGE-90|w]] sequence will establish owners, start controls and report progress to the oversight body
In the first phase the organisation should establish the facts, name owners and decide the order of work so every outstanding question is answered or assigned; the legal and compliance lead, fraud risk lead, HR and payroll lead, finance lead, IT lead and chief executive are the proposed owners for this stage, and the outcome is that every outstanding item has an owner and the order of work is recorded with its rationale. 
<!-- refs: STAGE-30 -->
In the second phase the organisation should approve the design, owner and record for each priority control and schedule its first operating cycle so the priority controls can begin to run; the HR and payroll lead, finance lead, commercial and customer lead, IT lead and the board or audit committee are proposed owners and the outcome is agreed designs and schedules for each priority control. 
<!-- refs: STAGE-60 -->
In the third phase the organisation should start operating the priority controls and report status to the oversight body so each priority control has run at least once and the board or its audit or risk committee has received a status update; the chief executive and the practical owners are accountable and the outcome is an operating record for each control and a board-level report.

# Leaders should close the verification gaps and settle outstanding questions within the first [[STAGE-90|w]] to reduce the most direct routes to loss
The organisation should recognise that the most direct routes to diverted funds and account takeover are operational and cross-functional, and that a focused set of verifications for payroll bank changes, new beneficiaries, customer detail changes, identity at hiring and help desk resets will reduce exposure while clarifying where further work should be directed. 
<!-- refs: THEME-VERIFY, N-PRIORITIES -->
Within [[STAGE-90|w]] the organisation should have assigned owners to every outstanding question, started the design and first operating cycle for the priority controls, and provided a first status report to the board or its audit or risk committee so leadership can judge whether further validation or investment is required; the sensible next step is for the named accountable executives to convene the first governance meeting, settle the outstanding answers and commit to the staged timetable.
