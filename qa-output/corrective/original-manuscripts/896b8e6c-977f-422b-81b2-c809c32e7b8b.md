# This report explains how the organisation’s answers translate into practical fraud control work

## The report moves from reported position to practical management action

This report sets out the organisation’s reported fraud control position, explains the management patterns arising from its answers, develops selected operational weaknesses, and describes the controls and governance actions that management can establish. It then provides a proposed sequence for assigning ownership, approving control designs, beginning operation and reporting progress to oversight.

# The reported position is shaped by uncertainty about controls and weak independent verification

## Functions do not always hold answers about their own controls, limiting management’s ability to direct response

The organisation’s answers include [[N-OUTSTANDING]] outstanding questions held across [[N-OUTSTANDING-FUNCTIONS]] functions. The affected topics include reporting, response, conflicts of interest, seniority exceptions, payment recall, customer blocking, loss tracking, payment instruction handling, security compromise response and speak-up arrangements. This means that management does not yet have a complete account of the practices that should support fraud prevention, detection and containment.
<!-- refs: THEME-UNCERTAINTY, N-OUTSTANDING, N-OUTSTANDING-FUNCTIONS -->

Uncertainty can affect response when a suspicious event requires rapid action. If a function cannot identify its escalation route, evidence source, decision-maker or communication procedure, an avoidable delay may occur while the organisation establishes who should act. Management should assign each outstanding matter to a named owner, obtain a clear answer or documented open item, and establish the operating record required for future oversight.
<!-- refs: THEME-UNCERTAINTY, STAGE-30 -->

## Requests that move money or change identity are not always verified through an independent route

The answers indicate that verification practices in people and payroll, customer activity, digital activity and finance may rely on information supplied by the requester. This can include a reply to the originating email, a call to a telephone number supplied in a request, or a callback made to a number selected by the caller. Such methods can appear persuasive while still being controlled by an impersonator.
<!-- refs: THEME-VERIFY, THEME-VERIFY-AREAS, THEME-VERIFY-NAREAS -->

Independent verification means the organisation uses a contact route, authoritative source or confirmation channel that the requester cannot direct. Examples within the reported issues include calling a known contact number, checking identity against Home Affairs records, requiring a manager confirmation through a known channel, and separating a customer change from sales activity. Leadership should require these routes to be used consistently wherever an instruction could move money, goods, identity details or account access.
<!-- refs: THEME-VERIFY, FINDING-01, FINDING-02, FINDING-03, FINDING-04, FINDING-05 -->

# Selected reported weaknesses show how routine processes could be exploited through impersonation

## Salary payments could be redirected where payroll bank changes are confirmed through the requester’s channel

The organisation reports that changes to employee bank details are confirmed by replying to the email or form that requested the change. This is a partly established arrangement because the confirmation route is connected to the original request rather than being initiated independently through information already held by the organisation.
<!-- refs: FINDING-01 -->

An impersonator could send a message appearing to come from an employee and provide replacement banking details. If the payroll process replies to that message or relies on the submitted form as confirmation, the impersonator may remain in control of the communication. A salary payment could then be directed to an account selected by the impersonator while the payroll transaction appears to relate to a genuine employee.
<!-- refs: FINDING-01, THEME-VERIFY -->

A sound operating arrangement requires payroll to use a channel it starts, such as a call to a contact number already on file or an in-person confirmation. The verification should occur before the amended details take effect, and the payroll process should retain evidence showing the channel used, the employee contacted, the result and any approved exception.
<!-- refs: FINDING-01, PRIORITY-01 -->

Management should direct the HR and payroll lead to establish the independent confirmation step and ensure that no bank detail change takes effect without it. The chief financial officer is accountable for the control response, while the practical owner should define the workflow, the evidence to retain and the approach to exceptions.
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-01 -->

[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-01]]

## False identities could enter employment where identity documents are retained without authoritative verification

The organisation reports that it takes a copy of a new employee’s identity document but does not check it against Home Affairs records. Retaining a document copy can support personnel administration, but it does not establish whether the document relates to the person presenting it or whether the identity information is valid.
<!-- refs: FINDING-02 -->

A person using a stolen, altered or invented identity could enter the hiring process and obtain employee status, payroll access or other legitimate credentials. Once a false identity has been embedded in ordinary records, later detection may be more difficult because the person may appear to hold a conventional employment history within the organisation.
<!-- refs: FINDING-02, THEME-VERIFY -->

Good practice is to verify each new employee’s identity against Home Affairs records, for example through a verification service, before the employee starts. The personnel file should contain the result of the verification and should identify any matter that requires follow-up before payroll, access or other onboarding steps proceed.
<!-- refs: FINDING-02, PRIORITY-02 -->

Management should require the HR and payroll lead to incorporate identity verification into the hiring workflow. The chief financial officer is accountable for the control response, and the practical owner should define the point at which verification is required, the evidence retained and the handling of an identity result that cannot be resolved.
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-02 -->

[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-02]]

## Fraudulent payment instructions could create a new beneficiary where confirmation relies on request details

The organisation reports that new beneficiary requests are confirmed by replying to the email or calling the number included in the request. This approach does not provide independent confirmation because an impersonator can control the email address, telephone number or both communication routes associated with the false instruction.
<!-- refs: FINDING-03 -->

An impersonator could submit an invoice or payment request that appears to come from a legitimate party and provide a beneficiary account under the impersonator’s control. Where finance uses the contact details in the request, the confirmation could be staged by the same person who initiated the request. A payment might then be released through an apparently complete approval process.
<!-- refs: FINDING-03, THEME-VERIFY -->

The expected control is for finance to call the requester through a number already held on file before releasing payment to a new beneficiary. The process should make clear that contact details supplied in the request cannot serve as the independent verification route, and the evidence of the call-back should be retained with the payment documentation.
<!-- refs: FINDING-03, PRIORITY-03 -->

Management should require the finance lead to establish a clear release condition for new beneficiary payments. The chief financial officer is accountable for the response, while the practical owner should define the call-back evidence, the authority for any exception and the steps to take where independent confirmation cannot be obtained.
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-03 -->

[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-03]]

## Customer funds or goods could be diverted where account changes are checked only when they appear unusual

The organisation reports that changes to customer refund accounts, delivery addresses and contact numbers are checked only where they appear unusual. A judgement-based approach can leave ordinary-looking fraudulent changes unchallenged, particularly where the change request contains plausible customer information.
<!-- refs: FINDING-04 -->

An attacker or fraudulent insider could alter a customer’s refund account, delivery address or contact number and allow the amended information to be used in a later transaction. A refund could be diverted or goods could be delivered to an unauthorised destination. The change might appear to be routine account maintenance unless the customer is contacted through details already held by the organisation.
<!-- refs: FINDING-04, THEME-VERIFY -->

A sound control requires every relevant customer change to be verified with the customer through contact details already on file. The change should also be made by a person outside sales, so that the person responsible for customer-facing activity is not solely responsible for changing information that can affect money or goods.
<!-- refs: FINDING-04, PRIORITY-04 -->

Management should direct the commercial and customer lead to design a workflow that identifies relevant changes, obtains independent customer confirmation and separates the change activity from sales. The chief financial officer is accountable for the response, and the practical owner should ensure that the evidence of verification is retained before the change takes effect.
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-04 -->

[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-04]]

## A compromised account could result where help desk resets are confirmed through a caller-controlled number

The organisation reports that the help desk calls a person back before resetting a password or multi-factor authentication, but uses a number provided by the caller. This is a partly established arrangement because the callback can be directed to the impersonator rather than to the genuine employee.
<!-- refs: FINDING-05 -->

An outsider claiming to be an employee could request a reset, provide a telephone number under the outsider’s control and complete the callback. If the reset is granted, the outsider may gain access to the employee account and use that legitimate identity to alter bank details, submit instructions or take actions available through the account.
<!-- refs: FINDING-05, PATHWAY-01 -->

Good practice is to verify every password or multi-factor reset through a route the caller does not control. This may include calling a known number already on file or obtaining manager confirmation through a known channel. The help desk should retain evidence of the verification route and the approval for any permitted exception.
<!-- refs: FINDING-05, PRIORITY-05 -->

Management should require the IT lead to stop relying on caller-supplied callback numbers and establish the independent verification requirement. The chief financial officer is accountable for the control response, and the practical owner should ensure that reset activity is logged in a form that supports later review.
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-05 -->

<!-- section: ESS-PATHWAYS -->
# An unverified help desk reset could provide access to an account used for financial activity

## A caller impersonating an employee could use a reset request to obtain legitimate account access

A plausible pathway begins when an outsider impersonates an employee and contacts the help desk or IT provider claiming that a password or authentication device is unavailable. The outsider requests a reset and supplies a contact number. If the help desk uses that number for confirmation, the outsider can answer the callback and present the request as legitimate.
<!-- refs: PATHWAY-01, FINDING-05 -->

After the reset, the outsider could sign in under the employee’s identity and use the access available to that account. Depending on the account permissions, this could include changing bank details or releasing payments. The weakness is compounded where systems that move money accept activity under the reset account without a separate strong sign-in requirement.
<!-- refs: PATHWAY-01 -->

The activity may appear to be ordinary work because it is performed under a genuine employee account. Warning indicators include a reset request claiming urgency or a lost device, a reset followed closely by payment or bank-detail activity, unusual sign-ins, or an employee who does not know that a reset was made. Containment involves locking the account, reversing changes where possible, contacting the bank regarding payment activity and preserving help desk and sign-in information.
<!-- refs: PATHWAY-01 -->

# The controls this report sets out first establish independent verification and protected reporting routes

## Payroll bank changes should require confirmation initiated by the organisation

The payroll bank-change control is included because the reported process confirms changes through the same email or form that requested them. That route could allow an impersonator to direct the apparent confirmation and divert salary payments without requiring access to the genuine employee’s established contact channel.
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-01, FINDING-01 -->

The organisation should establish confirmation through a channel it initiates, such as a call to a number already on file or an in-person confirmation, before a bank change takes effect. The chief financial officer is accountable and the HR and payroll lead is the practical owner. In the proposed middle stage, the intended measure is that each bank-detail change is independently confirmed before taking effect or is an approved and documented exception.
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-01, STAGE-60 -->

## Hiring should include identity verification against Home Affairs records

Identity verification is included because retaining an identity document copy does not establish that the new employee is the person represented by the document. A false or stolen identity could otherwise enter the organisation and obtain access through ordinary onboarding activity.
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-02, FINDING-02 -->

The organisation should require verification against Home Affairs records, for example through a verification service, before a new employee starts. The chief financial officer is accountable and the HR and payroll lead is the practical owner. In the proposed middle stage, the intended measure is that each new employee has an identity verification result before the start date.
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-02, STAGE-60 -->

## New beneficiary payments should require an independent call-back before release

New beneficiary confirmation is included because finance currently relies on contact details connected to the payment request. An impersonator could control those details and provide a false confirmation that appears to complete the finance process.
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-03, FINDING-03 -->

The organisation should require finance to call the requester through a number already held on file before releasing payment to a new beneficiary. The chief financial officer is accountable and the finance lead is the practical owner. In the proposed middle stage, the control target is that payment to a new beneficiary is independently confirmed before release.
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-03, STAGE-60 -->

## Customer detail changes should be verified independently and separated from sales activity

Customer detail verification is included because changes are currently checked only when they appear unusual. This leaves a route for a convincing but fraudulent request to alter refund accounts, delivery addresses or contact numbers without direct confirmation from the customer.
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-04, FINDING-04 -->

The organisation should verify each relevant customer change through details already on file and ensure that the change is made by someone outside sales. The chief financial officer is accountable and the commercial and customer lead is the practical owner. In the proposed middle stage, the intended measure is that each relevant change is independently verified before it takes effect.
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-04, STAGE-60 -->

## Help desk resets should use verification routes that callers cannot control

Help desk reset verification is included because a callback to a number supplied by the caller can confirm the identity of an impersonator rather than the employee. The resulting account access could then be used to carry out activity under a legitimate account identity.
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-05, FINDING-05 -->

The organisation should require a callback to a known number or manager confirmation through a separate known channel before resetting a password or multi-factor authentication. The chief financial officer is accountable and the IT lead is the practical owner. In the proposed middle stage, the intended measure is that each reset is independently confirmed before taking effect or is an approved and documented exception.
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-05, STAGE-60 -->

## Allegations involving senior people should have an independent written route

The independent allegation route is included because concerns involving executives or board members may be difficult to raise through ordinary reporting channels. An accessible route to an independent recipient supports appropriate handling where the subject of a concern may otherwise influence the normal escalation process.
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-06, AREA-D1-GAP -->

The organisation should establish a written route for allegations about any executive or board member and ensure that people know it exists. The chief executive is accountable and the board or audit committee is the practical owner. In the proposed middle stage, the intended control target is a written, understood route that directs such allegations to an independent recipient.
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-06, STAGE-60 -->

# The proposed staged response moves from clarification to approved design and operating evidence

## The initial proposed stage should establish ownership, answers and an agreed order of work

The initial proposed stage is intended to establish the facts required to direct the response. The organisation should answer each outstanding question or retain an open item with a named owner, identify an accountable executive for each open area and document the factors used to decide the order of work. Legal and compliance, fraud risk, HR and payroll, finance, IT and the chief executive have roles in this work.
<!-- refs: STAGE-30, N-OUTSTANDING, N-OPEN-AREAS -->

## The proposed middle stage should approve control designs and prepare their operating cycles

The proposed middle stage is intended to approve the design, owner and evidence requirement for the selected controls. This includes payroll bank-change confirmation, identity verification, new beneficiary confirmation, customer detail verification, help desk reset verification and the independent route for allegations involving senior people. The relevant practical owners should prepare each control for operation and oversight.
<!-- refs: STAGE-60, N-PRIORITIES -->

## The proposed later stage should begin operation and provide status to oversight

The proposed later stage is intended to move from design into operating practice. Each selected control should have operated and retained supporting evidence, while management should provide a status report to the board or its audit or risk committee. This gives the oversight body a basis for considering progress, unresolved matters and any further action required.

<!-- section: ESS-CONCLUSION -->
# Management should establish independent verification and clearer ownership before relying on the current control environment

## The practical response is to settle uncertainty and make sensitive changes harder to manipulate

The organisation should recognise that its answers describe widespread reported control gaps alongside unanswered questions about important prevention, detection and response arrangements. The immediate management task is to allocate ownership for the unresolved matters and establish reliable operating practices for instructions that affect money, identity, customer information or account access.
<!-- refs: THEME-UNCERTAINTY, THEME-VERIFY, N-GAP, N-OUTSTANDING -->

If management completes the proposed stages, the organisation should have clearer ownership for open matters, approved designs for the selected controls, evidence from their operation and a status report for oversight. The sensible next step is for the chief executive and accountable executives to agree the ownership of each open matter, approve the required control designs and ensure that the board or its audit or risk committee receives progress information.
