# The uncertainty about who knows what and verification practices that accept requester evidence explain most of the position

## Several functions do not hold answers that limit certainty about controls

Several functions answered that they did not know whether key practices operate as described, producing outstanding questions about suspicious transaction reporting, response decisions, declaration of interests, rapid recall procedures, loss tracking, verification for communication modes and the existence of a single register for allegations; these unknowns reduce confidence in the completeness of the control picture and require named owners to settle them.  
<!-- refs: THEME-UNCERTAINTY, N-OUTSTANDING -->

## Verification practices accept requester evidence across multiple functions and create immediate payment and identity risks

Across payroll, customer account management, finance and IT help desk practices the organisation reports that changes and resets are confirmed by channels the requester controls, such as replying to an initiating email or using a phone number provided in a request, which allows impersonation or altered contact details to be used to redirect payments or grant access.

<!-- section: ESS-FINDINGS -->
# The findings that follow show the specific control gaps the report develops and why they matter

The selection rule for these findings is the method's rule: scenarios with answers to settle or questions to clarify, then scenarios chosen from each theme for the number of core controls reported absent and related practices reported weaker.  
<!-- refs: N-FINDINGS, N-FINDINGS-REGISTER -->

## Salaries can be redirected through bank changes confirmed only by reply to the requester

The organisation reports that changes to employee bank details are confirmed by replying to the email or form that requested them rather than by a channel the organisation starts, which means an impersonator who controls the initiating channel could redirect salaries; HR and payroll leadership should require confirmation through a route the organisation starts before any change takes effect to reduce the risk of salary redirection.  
<!-- refs: FINDING-01, AREA-D5-GAP -->

[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-01]]

## A false identity can enter at hiring because identity documents are copied but not verified against authoritative records

The reported practice is that a copy of an identity document is taken at hiring but not checked against Home Affairs records or a verification service, which permits the risk that ghost or fictitious employees are created or that false credentials are accepted at hiring; HR and payroll leadership should verify new employees' identity against authoritative records before they start.  
<!-- refs: FINDING-02, AREA-D5-GAP -->

[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-02]]

## A new beneficiary can be paid on a fake instruction because new‑payee confirmations use the requester's contact details

Finance reports that new beneficiary requests are confirmed by replying to the email or calling the number in the request rather than calling a number already on record, which allows an impersonator to supply their own contact and receive payment; finance leadership should confirm new beneficiaries through a call‑back to a number already on file before release.  
<!-- refs: FINDING-03, AREA-D6-GAP -->

[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-03]]

## Customer details can be changed to divert money or goods because changes are checked only if they look unusual

Changes to refund accounts, delivery addresses or contact numbers are currently verified only when they appear unusual, which creates opportunities for diverted refunds, goods or payments when changes appear routine; commercial and customer leadership should require verification with the customer using details already on file and have changes made by someone outside sales.  
<!-- refs: FINDING-04, AREA-D7-GAP -->

[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-04]]

## A help desk reset can hand an attacker account access because the help desk calls back to a number the caller provides

The IT help desk reports that it calls the requester back to a number the caller gives rather than to a number on file, which enables social engineering of resets and subsequent account takeover; IT leadership should implement verifications that use routes the caller cannot control to prevent unauthorised resets that lead to account takeover.  
<!-- refs: FINDING-05, AREA-D9-GAP -->

[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-05]]

<!-- section: ESS-PATHWAYS -->
# A help desk reset can hand an attacker the account that moves money and so enable payment fraud

## A help desk reset hands an attacker the account that moves money, enabling payments to be released under a legitimate account

An impersonator contacts the help desk and persuades staff to reset a password or multi‑factor authentication using caller‑supplied contact details, then signs in as the employee and uses the account to change bank details or approve payments; the weakness relied on is that resets are verified through routes the caller controls and that systems that move money accept the reset account, and the consequence is payments or changes carried out under a legitimate user that complicate detection and proof.  
<!-- refs: PATHWAY-01, THEME-VERIFY -->

<!-- section: ESS-PRIORITIES -->
# The first [[N-PRIORITIES|w]] responses set out the controls that stop the selected fraud scenarios

Leadership should design and implement the priority controls that directly address the findings: confirmation of employee bank changes through organisation‑started channels; identity verification of new hires against authoritative records; call‑back confirmation of new beneficiaries to numbers already on file; verification of customer detail changes with customers and segregation from sales; verification of help desk resets through routes the caller cannot control; and a written route for allegations about senior people to an independent recipient, with each control owned, designed and scheduled to run.

# Within the proposed programme leadership should settle outstanding answers, put the priority controls in place and report their first cycles to oversight

Leadership should recognise that settling the outstanding questions is the immediate enabler of the control work, that implementing the priority verification and help desk controls will close the direct pathways to diverted pay and account takeover, and that owners should be appointed to deliver and record those first operating cycles.  
<!-- refs: STAGE-90, PRIORITY-03 -->

A sensible next step is for the oversight body to ask for a short status report after the initial and intermediate windows showing answers settled, priority control designs approved, owners appointed and a schedule for the first operating cycles, so that the board or its audit or risk committee can track progress and decide further resourcing or assurance.
