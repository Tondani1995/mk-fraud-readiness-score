# Selected operational weaknesses show how impersonation can affect payroll, hiring, payments, customer changes and account access

## Salary bank-detail changes can redirect pay when confirmation follows the requesting channel

The organisation reports that employee bank-detail changes are confirmed by replying to the email or form that requested the change. This does not provide confirmation through a route initiated by the organisation, because the requester may control the email address, the form submission or the contact information associated with the request.
<!-- refs: FINDING-01, BLUEPRINT-01 -->

A person impersonating an employee, or using access to an employee’s email, could submit an apparently routine request to change salary banking details. If HR or payroll replies through the same route, the impersonator can respond to the confirmation. The change could then take effect before the genuine employee becomes aware that their salary destination has been altered.
<!-- refs: FINDING-01, THEME-VERIFY -->

The target arrangement is that every employee bank-detail change is confirmed through a channel the organisation starts, such as a call to a number already held by the organisation or an in-person discussion. The HR and payroll lead should retain a change log showing the request, the confirmation method, the person completing it and the point at which the change took effect.
<!-- refs: BLUEPRINT-01, MEASURE-01, ROLE-OWNERS -->

The chief financial officer should require the revised procedure to be introduced during the proposed Establish stage. Access to make payroll changes should be limited, any unconfirmed change should be suspended, and the fraud risk lead should sample the log and report exceptions so that management can identify whether the procedure is being followed.

## Hiring can admit a false identity when document copies are not checked against an authoritative source

The organisation reports that identity document copies are collected for new employees but are not checked. A document image on its own does not establish that the person presenting it is the individual named in the document or that the document is genuine.

## Help desk resets can give an impersonator access when the caller supplies the confirmation number

The organisation reports that the help desk calls a person back when a password or multi-factor reset is requested, but uses the number supplied by the caller. This means that a person falsely claiming to be an employee can direct the help desk to a number under the attacker’s control.

<!-- section: COMP-PATHWAYS -->
# A compromised help desk reset can create a route from impersonation to financial or account changes

## An attacker can use a fraudulent reset to act through a legitimate employee account

An outsider may impersonate an employee to the help desk or an IT provider, claiming a lost phone or urgent access problem. Where the reset confirmation is sent to a number supplied by the caller, the attacker can control both the initial request and the supposed confirmation, allowing a password or multi-factor reset to be completed.
<!-- refs: PATHWAY-01, FINDING-05 -->

After the reset, the attacker may sign in using the employee’s account and use access available to that account to change banking details or release payments. The weakness becomes more consequential where systems that move money accept actions under the reset account without a further strong sign-in or independent confirmation of the sensitive transaction.
<!-- refs: PATHWAY-01, THEME-VERIFY -->

The activity may appear to have been performed by a genuine employee because it occurs under a legitimate account. Warning signs include a reset associated with urgency or a lost phone, activity affecting payments or bank details shortly after a reset, sign-ins from an unusual device or location, or an employee who does not know that a reset was made in their name.
<!-- refs: PATHWAY-01 -->

If the pathway is suspected, the organisation should lock the affected account, reverse relevant changes, contact the bank about any payment at risk and preserve help desk and sign-in information. The longer-term control response is to require independent reset confirmation and stronger sign-in protection for systems that move money.

<!-- section: COMP-CONCLUSION -->
# Management can strengthen fraud control by settling uncertainty, assigning ownership and introducing independent confirmation for sensitive changes

The organisation’s answers show a limited set of scenarios with every core control reported in place and a broader set where core controls are reported as gaps. The required transition is practical: management needs to establish unanswered facts, decide who owns each open scenario and introduce control steps that prevent a requester from controlling the confirmation of a sensitive instruction.
<!-- refs: N-REPORTED-HELD, N-APPLY, N-GAP, N-OUTSTANDING, THEME-VERIFY -->

The proposed Establish stage concentrates on bank-detail changes, identity checks, new-beneficiary payments, customer-detail changes and help desk resets. These control designs are connected by the use of independent contact routes, source-based identity checks, clear process ownership, operational logs and exception escalation.
<!-- refs: PHASE-ESTABLISH, BLUEPRINT-01, BLUEPRINT-02, BLUEPRINT-03, BLUEPRINT-04, BLUEPRINT-05 -->

The chief executive should ensure that accountable executives and process owners have the authority to make the required procedural and access changes. The fraud risk lead should coordinate shared requirements and consolidate management information, while the board or its audit or risk committee should receive status at every meeting until open scenarios have reached a management decision.
<!-- refs: ROLE-EXECUTIVE, ROLE-RISK, ROLE-OVERSIGHT, DECISION-04 -->

By the proposed Mature stage, management should have measures from full operating cycles, a clearer understanding of outstanding matters and a routine for escalating exceptions. This will allow the oversight body to consider progress and decide whether remaining scenarios should be addressed, deferred with a reason or accepted.
<!-- refs: PHASE-MATURE, MEASURE-CYCLES, MEASURE-OWNERS, MEASURE-REPORT -->

A separate advisory engagement may add value after management has implemented its chosen controls and wishes to consider evidence review. Until then, this report remains a practical interpretation of the organisation’s own answers and a guide to the control and governance actions management may take.
