# Uncertainty about operating ownership and verification-by-requester are the management patterns that drive exposure

## Several functions cannot confirm core operating practices and that uncertainty constrains remedial action
A number of functions answered that they do not know whether specific operating practices are in place for matters such as suspicious transaction reporting, rapid recall procedures, loss tracking and verification steps for customer and payment changes, which means leadership lacks the factual basis to design, sequence and assure controls. 
<!-- refs: THEME-UNCERTAINTY, N-OUTSTANDING, N-OUTSTANDING-FUNCTIONS -->

The management implication is that design work and training will be harder to scope and sustain until named owners answer those questions; therefore the proposed first stage of work is to settle the outstanding items, assign clear accountable executives and record the order of work against facts. 
<!-- refs: THEME-UNCERTAINTY, STAGE-30 -->

## Changes that move money or alter identity are often accepted on the requester’s word across multiple processes
The answers describe a practical pattern where bank detail changes, beneficiary additions, customer account updates and help desk resets are confirmed by replying to the originating message or by using contact details provided in the request, rather than by verification through a route the requester does not control; this creates a consistent opportunity for impersonation and diversion. 
<!-- refs: THEME-VERIFY, THEME-VERIFY-N, THEME-VERIFY-AREAS, THEME-VERIFY-NAREAS -->

For leaders, the control implication is straightforward: requiring verification through routes the organisation starts for these change types will close a family of related vulnerabilities and deliver defensive value across payroll, finance, customer operations and IT.

# Five operational weaknesses explain most of the immediate fraud exposure and merit leadership attention

## Salaries are redirected through a fake bank change when confirmations are made by replying to the request
On its answers, the organisation describes bank change confirmations being performed by replying to the email or form that requested the change, with the HR and payroll lead named as the practical owner of that workflow. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-01 -->

That operating approach allows a straightforward impersonation or account compromise vector: an attacker who spoofs an employee email, persuades an internal user, or gains control of an inbox can submit or amend a bank change and rely on the reply‑based confirmation to make the change effective. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-01, THEME-VERIFY -->

Good practice is to treat every change to an employee’s bank details as requiring confirmation through a route the organisation initiates, for example a call to a number already on file or an in person confirmation, to record the confirmation details and to log any approved exceptions against documented authority. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-01, FINDING-01 -->

The pragmatic first step is for HR and payroll to treat any bank change received through email or an incoming form as an exception until the change is verified by an organisation‑started route, to record the exceptions and to provide an initial compliance report to the chief financial officer within [[STAGE-60]]. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-01, STAGE-60 -->

[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-01]]

## A false identity can enter at hiring because identity documents are taken but not checked against authoritative records
The organisation reports that recruitment collects copies of identity documents but does not verify those documents against Home Affairs records or a verification service, with the HR and payroll lead responsible for the hiring process. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-02 -->

Accepting an unverified identity at hiring creates exposures such as fictitious employees on the payroll, false credentials being used to establish access and long‑lived insider risk that is hard to detect once payroll and IT access are in place. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-02 -->

A practical control approach is to require identity verification for every new starter against an authoritative source before the person starts, for example through a recognised verification service, and to store the verification result in the personnel record so payroll and access provisioning rely on a single, auditable truth. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-02, FINDING-02 -->

The immediate action is for HR to make identity verification a mandatory pre‑start task, to adopt or contract a verification service, and to deliver a short rollout plan and evidence of design to the chief financial officer during [[STAGE-60]]. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-02, STAGE-60 -->

[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-02]]

## A new beneficiary can be paid on a fake instruction because confirmations use contact details from the request
Finance describes confirming new beneficiary requests by replying to the originating email or by calling the number supplied in the request, and the finance lead is the practical owner of the payment workflow. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-03 -->

When confirmation relies on contact information supplied by the requester, an attacker who submits a fraudulent invoice or alters supplier details can provide contact numbers that route the verification to a controlled endpoint, which makes it likely that the payment will be released to the attacker’s account. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-03, THEME-VERIFY -->

A robust control is to require a call‑back to a number already on record before any payment to a new beneficiary is released, to log the call details, the person spoken to and the authority that allowed any exception, and to ensure that the payment system only accepts confirmations recorded against the original supplier record. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-03, FINDING-03 -->

The first step for Finance is to adopt the call‑back rule as policy, to pilot the workflow for the first operating cycle, to maintain a simple confirmation log and to report results and exceptions to the chief financial officer within [[STAGE-60]]. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-03, STAGE-60 -->

[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-03]]

## Customer details can be changed without independent verification and that enables diversion of refunds or goods
Commercial and customer staff report that changes to customer contact details or refund accounts are only checked when they appear unusual, and those changes can be initiated within commercial teams rather than by a separate verification function. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-04 -->

This arrangement allows a plausible route for diversion: refunds, credit notes or order deliveries can be redirected by altering bank accounts, addresses or contact numbers without independent customer confirmation, and staged or repeated small changes can conceal the diversion over time. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-04 -->

A more resilient approach is to verify every change to a customer’s refund account, delivery address or contact numbers with the customer using details already on file, and to ensure that the verification is performed by a function outside sales so that incentives and access do not conflict with control. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-04, FINDING-04 -->

As a practical first action the commercial and customer lead should require recorded verification for every customer detail change, document the verification method, log exceptions and provide a first-cycle status update to the accountable executive during [[STAGE-60]]. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-04, STAGE-60 -->

[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-04]]

## A help desk reset can hand an attacker access because call‑backs use numbers the caller supplies
IT reports that help desk staff verify password and multi‑factor resets by calling back to the number the caller provides instead of using a number already on file or a manager confirmation, and IT is the practical owner of the reset process. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-05 -->

That practice enables social engineering where an attacker claims a lost device or urgency, supplies a callback number that routes to the attacker, obtains a reset, takes control of an employee account and uses that legitimate account to change payment details or approve transactions. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-05, PATHWAY-01 -->

Best practice is to require resets to be verified through a route the organisation initiates, for example a call to a number on the personnel record, a manager confirmation through a known channel or an automated out‑of‑band mechanism, and to log the verification action for audit. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-05, FINDING-05 -->

The immediate step is for IT to change operating procedure so that every password or multi‑factor reset is confirmed through a route the organisation starts, to train service desk staff, and to report the first operating outcomes to the chief executive and the oversight body during [[STAGE-60]]. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-05, STAGE-60 -->

[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-05]]

<!-- section: ESS-PATHWAYS -->
# A help desk reset verified on the caller’s number can hand an attacker the account that moves money

## An impersonator uses the help desk to gain control of a legitimate account and then moves money
An outsider impersonates an employee to the help desk, supplies a callback number that they control, and persuades the service desk to reset the password or multi‑factor authentication on that basis, which allows the attacker to sign in as the employee. 
<!-- refs: PATHWAY-01 -->

Because systems that effect payments accept the employee account for authorisation and because the reset route is controlled by the attacker, the attacker can change bank details or approve payments under a legitimate identity, which both conceals the activity and complicates forensic attribution. 
<!-- refs: PATHWAY-01, FINDING-05 -->

Warning indicators include an urgent reset request claiming a lost device, a reset immediately followed by payment or bank‑detail changes, sign‑ins from unusual places, or an employee denying a reset made in their name; containment should lock the account, reverse changes where possible, preserve help desk and sign‑in logs and notify the bank and regulators as appropriate. 
<!-- refs: PATHWAY-01 -->

<!-- section: ESS-PRIORITIES -->
# The short list of first responses should settle unknowns and put in the controls that close the selected findings

## Bank changes must be confirmed by a channel the organisation starts
Establishing that every change to an employee’s bank details is confirmed through a route the organisation initiates directly addresses the payroll redirection exposure and removes a routine impersonation vector. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-01, FINDING-01 -->

Accountable and practical owners should define the confirmation procedure, embed it in the payroll workflow, train HR staff, require recorded evidence of confirmation on file and schedule the first operating cycle for review; the stage for this work is [[STAGE-60]] and the chief financial officer is named as accountable. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-01, STAGE-60 -->

## Every new employee’s identity must be verified before they start
Requiring identity verification against an authoritative source prior to start prevents fictitious hires, strengthens payroll integrity and reduces the risk of false identities gaining persistent access. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-02, FINDING-02 -->

The HR and payroll lead should adopt a recognised verification service or Home Affairs check, make verification mandatory for onboarding, store results in the personnel file and report compliance to the chief financial officer; this work is scheduled for [[STAGE-60]]. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-02, STAGE-60 -->

## New beneficiaries must be confirmed by calling a number already on record
Requiring a callback to a number already on file before releasing any payment to a new beneficiary closes a standard invoice diversion route and ensures the confirmation is made to an established supplier contact. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-03, FINDING-03 -->

Finance should update its payment releases policy, keep a simple log of confirmations and exceptions, seek practical automation where possible, and provide a first-cycle report to the chief financial officer after [[STAGE-60]]. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-03, STAGE-60 -->

## Customer detail changes must be verified with the customer through details already on file
Every change to a customer’s refund account, delivery address or contact numbers should be verified with the customer using details already on record, and the verification should be performed by a function outside sales to avoid conflicts of interest. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-04, FINDING-04 -->

The commercial and customer lead should design a documented verification workflow, log every verification and exception, train staff and report operating results to the accountable executive within [[STAGE-60]]. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-04, STAGE-60 -->

## Help desk resets must be confirmed through a route the organisation does not allow the caller to control
Changing the help desk procedure so that callbacks use numbers already on file, a manager’s confirmation through a known channel or an automated out‑of‑band mechanism eliminates a large social engineering vector and protects credentials used to move money. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-05, FINDING-05 -->

IT should update standard operating procedures, train service desk staff, log reset confirmations and exceptions, and present the first operating data to the chief executive and oversight body during [[STAGE-60]]. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-05, STAGE-60 -->

## There must be a written route for allegations about senior people to an independent recipient
A formally documented route that directs allegations involving executives or board members to an independent recipient reduces the risk that concerns are lost or handled informally in ways that leave senior conduct unexamined. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-06 -->

The board or its audit committee should endorse the route, publicise it inside the organisation, confirm operational independence and report that the route is in effect during [[STAGE-60]], with the chief executive accountable and the board or audit committee the practical owner. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-06, STAGE-60 -->

<!-- section: ESS-CONCLUSION -->
# Leaders should settle the unknowns, require the verification controls that stop simple impersonation and report the first operating cycle to the oversight body

## A few procedural changes will materially reduce the routine exposure and create a basis for further work
Leaders should recognise that the principal practical weakness is procedural: a set of operating practices accepts changes that move money or change identity on the requester’s word while several functions cannot confirm their own controls, and together those conditions create routine, preventable exposure across payroll, payments, customer and IT processes. 
<!-- refs: THEME-VERIFY, THEME-UNCERTAINTY -->

The sensible next steps are to settle outstanding questions, implement verification routes the organisation initiates for bank and beneficiary changes, require identity checks at hiring, tighten help desk reset verification and then report the first operating cycle results to the board or its audit or risk committee so leadership can decide further work and resourcing.
