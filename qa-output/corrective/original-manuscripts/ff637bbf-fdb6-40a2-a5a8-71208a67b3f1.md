# This report explains what the assessment found and what to do first

## The report summarises the organisation's answers, the patterns that drive them and the first responses management should consider
This document describes the organisation's answers to the assessment questionnaire, explains the management-level patterns that arise from those answers, develops a set of advisory findings and proposes the first responses and a proposed first ninety day plan for management to consider.

# The organisation reports [[N-APPLY]] applicable fraud scenarios and core controls in place for [[N-REPORTED-HELD]]

## The answers show that most applicable scenarios report core controls as gaps and several scenarios remain unanswered
On its own answers, the organisation reports every core control in place for a small subset of the applicable scenarios while the majority of scenarios are described with a core control reported as a gap and a set of scenarios remain with outstanding answers, which together indicate that many preventive, detective and response controls are not yet established across processes. 
<!-- refs: N-APPLY, N-REPORTED-HELD, N-GAP, N-OUTSTANDING-SCHEMES -->

## The operating pattern is driven by absent verification routes that the requester controls and by uncertainty about control ownership
A clear cross-cutting pattern is that controls which require verification by a route the organisation initiates, for example callbacks to numbers on file or independent identity checks, are commonly absent or weakened across functions, and that several functions stated they did not know whether key controls operate as described, which together create both the operational opportunities fraud actors favour and an uncertainty that slows confident planning. 
<!-- refs: THEME-VERIFY, THEME-UNCERTAINTY, N-OUTSTANDING, N-OUTSTANDING-FUNCTIONS -->

## Management should settle open questions quickly and prioritise control designs that prevent requests being acted on the requester's word
The most direct first steps are to resolve outstanding questions that are quick to answer, appoint accountable executives for open areas, and to implement the priority controls that stop routine requests being converted into payments or identity changes by requiring organisation-initiated verification before sensitive actions proceed. 
<!-- refs: THEME-UNCERTAINTY, THEME-VERIFY, N-OUTSTANDING -->

## No score or approved readiness conclusion is issued with this report and management should treat the material as advisory
A scoring method has not been approved for this assessment and no readiness conclusion is drawn; the report therefore presents an advisory interpretation of the organisation's answers and a proposed order of work rather than an assurance or tested rating.

## Several functions do not hold answers about their own controls and leadership should settle those uncertainties first
Multiple functions replied that they did not know whether particular controls operate as described, leaving questions about incident notification, suspicious transaction reporting, human review, verification for non-written channels and other matters unresolved; resolving these items will materially change the plan and should be one of the first tasks the leadership team commissions.

## Instructions that move money or change identity are often accepted on the requester's word and that must be closed by organisation-started verification
A cross-area pattern is that changes to payment details, beneficiary records, identity and access are frequently accepted through routes the requester controls, such as replies to email or numbers supplied in requests, which creates straightforward social engineering paths for fraud; the corrective choice is to introduce verification routes the organisation starts for these actions. 
<!-- refs: THEME-VERIFY, THEME-VERIFY-N, THEME-VERIFY-AREAS, THEME-VERIFY-NAREAS -->

Leadership implication: the board or oversight committee should require that the priority controls establish a single rule set for organisation-started verification across HR, finance, customer operations and IT, so that the same principle is applied consistently where it matters most.

<!-- section: ESS-FINDINGS -->
# The report develops [[N-FINDINGS|w]] findings that deserve early leadership attention

## Salaries can be redirected because bank changes are confirmed by reply rather than by an organisation-started route
The organisation states that changes to employee bank details are confirmed by replying to the email or form that requested them rather than by using a verification route the organisation initiates, which leaves payroll vulnerable to a forged change request or an intercepted communication being accepted as genuine. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-01, AREA-D5-GAP, THEME-VERIFY -->

In operational terms an attacker can create or intercept a change request and rely on the reply confirmation process to effect the alteration without needing to compromise HR or payroll systems, and because payroll cycles are often automated a diverted payment can be executed before staff detect the mismatch. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-01, PRIORITY-01, AREA-D5-GAP -->

Good practice is to require a verification step that the organisation initiates before any bank-detail change takes effect, such as a callback to a number already on file, an in-person confirmation or an identity verification check, with the verification result logged in the payroll record and retained for audit and reconciliation. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-01, PRIORITY-01 -->

The immediate management action is to instruct HR and payroll to suspend reliance on reply confirmations, document the new verification route, assign a practical owner to implement the control and schedule the detailed design for the proposed second stage so the change cannot be applied without the new verification. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-01, PRIORITY-01 -->

[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-01]]

## A false identity can enter at hiring because identity documents are taken but not checked against authoritative records
The organisation reports that a copy of an identity document is taken at hiring but that the copy is not checked against Home Affairs or a similar authoritative source, which leaves the recruitment process open to phantom or falsified identities and to incorrect credentials being accepted into personnel and payroll records. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-02, AREA-D5-GAP, THEME-VERIFY -->

From an abuse perspective, a person using a stolen or fabricated identity can obtain system access and payroll status or enable a fictitious payee to be created, and once the individual has started and obtained credentials it becomes operationally complex to identify and remove the false identity without disrupting legitimate services. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-02, PRIORITY-02, AREA-D5-GAP -->

Good practice is to perform identity verification against an authoritative source for every new hire before they start, for example by using a Home Affairs verification service or equivalent, to record the verification outcome in the personnel file, and to prevent system access and payroll activation until the verification result is available. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-02, PRIORITY-02 -->

The first step for management is to instruct HR to define the pre-start identity verification requirement, to select an appropriate verification supplier or service, to update recruitment checklists and to schedule the control for the proposed second stage so that starters cannot be provisioned until the check is complete. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-02, PRIORITY-02 -->

[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-02]]

## Payments to new beneficiaries can be released on a caller-supplied contact rather than by an independent callback
Finance reports that requests to add or change beneficiaries are validated by replying to the email or calling the number supplied in the request, which means a fraud actor who controls the request channel can specify a contact that routes confirmations to accounts under their control. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-03, AREA-D6-GAP, THEME-VERIFY -->

In practice an impersonator can submit a forged invoice or compromise a supplier email account and then rely on the supplied contact details to confirm the change, so a payment can be wired to an account the impostor controls and recovery becomes difficult after funds leave the banking system. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-03, PRIORITY-03, AREA-D6-GAP -->

Good practice is to require that every payment to a new beneficiary is confirmed by a call-back to a telephone number that is already on record for the requester or by using another independent, organisation-controlled contact route, to log the callback outcome in the payment file and to refuse release until the callback is recorded. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-03, PRIORITY-03 -->

To reduce near-term exposure the finance lead should be instructed to stop accepting confirmations by reply or by caller-supplied numbers, to define the call-back procedure, to update the payment release checklist and to implement the change in the proposed second stage so that all new-beneficiary payments require an organisation-initiated confirmation. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-03, PRIORITY-03 -->

[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-03]]

## Customer details can be changed without systematic verification and that allows diversion of refunds and goods
The organisation states changes to customer refund accounts, delivery addresses and contact numbers are checked only when staff consider them unusual, which leaves routine change requests to staff judgement and increases the chance that legitimate looking requests will be actioned without independent customer confirmation. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-04, AREA-D7-GAP, THEME-VERIFY -->

A practical exploitation is that an attacker persuades a customer-facing contact to accept a routine-looking change and then collects refunds or diverts shipments to addresses used to launder goods, and because sales teams typically handle customer conversations the lack of an independent verification step increases the risk that customers are unaware of the change. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-04, PRIORITY-04, AREA-D7-GAP -->

Good practice is to verify any change to a customer's refund account, delivery address or contact numbers by contacting the customer using details already on file and to require that such changes are executed by a function independent of the sales team, with the verification outcome logged and available for review. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-04, PRIORITY-04 -->

The immediate management instruction is to require that customer detail changes be held pending verification via the customer’s existing contact details, to appoint an independent operational owner for these changes and to plan for the implementation in the proposed second stage so that customer-facing teams cannot complete a change on their own. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-04, PRIORITY-04 -->

[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-04]]

## The help desk verifies resets by calling a number the caller provides, which can hand an attacker account control
IT states that password and multi-factor resets are verified by calling the person back on the number the caller gives rather than by using a route the organisation initiates, which makes resets vulnerable to social engineering when the attacker supplies a callback number they control. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-05, AREA-D9-GAP, THEME-VERIFY -->

An attacker who persuades the help desk to perform a reset and receives the callback can sign in as the legitimate user and then make changes to bank details, release payments or access sensitive customer or financial records, and because activity appears under a genuine account this can delay detection and complicate forensic analysis. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-05, PATHWAY-01, AREA-D9-GAP -->

Best practice is to require help desk resets to be verified through a route the organisation starts, for example a callback to a number already on file, or by a manager confirmation through a separate known channel, and to log the request, the verification route and the outcome so resets are auditable and anomalous patterns can be detected. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-05, PRIORITY-05 -->

The first practical step is to instruct IT to cease accepting caller-supplied numbers for callbacks, to formalise the organisation-initiated verification route, to update help desk procedures and to schedule the change for the proposed second stage so that resets require independent verification before credentials are changed. 
<!-- refs: FINDING-05, PRIORITY-05 -->

<!-- section: ESS-PATHWAYS -->
# A credible pathway shows how a help desk reset can deliver the account that moves money

## An impersonator can obtain a reset, sign in as the employee and then use their privileges to change bank details or release payments
An attacker impersonating an employee to the help desk can request a password or multi-factor reset, receive the reset because verification was performed on a number the attacker controls, sign in as the employee and then exercise whatever authorisations the account holds to change bank details or approve payments, translating social engineering into an operational loss. 
<!-- refs: PATHWAY-01, FINDING-05, THEME-VERIFY -->

The pathway is enabled by two weaknesses acting together: help desk verification that relies on caller-supplied contact details, and payment or beneficiary processes that allow sensitive changes or releases under standard credentials without an additional, organisation-started verification step; the attacker benefits from concealment because activity is logged under a legitimate identity. 
<!-- refs: PATHWAY-01, AREA-D9-GAP, AREA-D6-GAP -->

Warning signs include a reset request claiming a lost device or an unusual urgency, followed quickly by a payment or bank-detail activity, sign-ins from unfamiliar locations or devices, or an employee reporting an unexpected reset; immediate containment is to lock the affected account, reverse unauthorised changes where possible, contact the bank about any payment and preserve help desk and sign-in logs for investigation. 
<!-- refs: PATHWAY-01, PRIORITY-05, PRIORITY-03 -->

# The priority controls are the fastest way to close the selected findings and to settle unanswered questions

## Bank changes must be confirmed through an organisation-started route and HR must implement it
Bank changes confirmed with the employee is the priority control to prevent salary redirection, and management should establish as a rule that every change to an employee's bank details is confirmed through a route the organisation initiates before the change takes effect. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-01, FINDING-01 -->

To operationalise this control the chief financial officer is accountable, HR and payroll are the practical owners, and the organisation should define the allowed verification routes, log the verification outcome in the payroll file and treat any exception as a documented, approved and time-limited deviation; schedule the design and approvals in the proposed second stage. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-01 -->

## Identity verification must be done against authoritative records before a new employee starts
Identity verified is the priority control to prevent false identities entering at hiring, and the organisation should require that every new employee's identity be verified against Home Affairs or a similar authoritative source before they start work or are granted system access. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-02, FINDING-02 -->

Practically this means HR must select an identity verification service, update recruitment workflows to block provisioning until verification is complete, keep the verification result in the personnel record and monitor compliance; the chief financial officer is accountable and HR and payroll are the practical owners, with implementation scheduled for the proposed second stage. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-02 -->

## New beneficiary payments must be confirmed by calling a number already on record
New beneficiaries confirmed by call-back is the priority control to stop payments to fraudulent accounts, and finance should require that every payment to a new beneficiary be confirmed by calling a requester contact already on record or by an equivalent organisation-controlled channel before release. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-03, FINDING-03 -->

Finance should update payment request forms, maintain an authorised contact registry, log every callback outcome in the payment file and enforce that no new-beneficiary payment is released without a recorded confirmation; the chief financial officer is accountable and the finance lead is the practical owner, and the control should be designed and approved in the proposed second stage. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-03 -->

## All customer detail changes must be verified with existing contact details and be processed outside sales
Customer detail changes verified is the priority control to prevent diversion of refunds and deliveries, and the organisation should verify changes to refund accounts, delivery addresses and contact numbers by contacting the customer through details already on file and by requiring the change to be processed by a function outside the sales team. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-04, FINDING-04 -->

Implementing this control requires a handover mechanism from sales to an independent operational team, a verification checklist, a log of verification outcomes and supervisory review of changes that are material or flagged; the chief financial officer is accountable and the commercial and customer lead is the practical owner, with work scheduled for the proposed second stage. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-04 -->

## Help desk resets must be verified through an organisation-started route and logged
Help desk resets verified is the priority control to reduce account takeover risk, and the organisation should require that every password or multi-factor reset be confirmed through a route the organisation starts, such as a callback to a number on file or manager confirmation through a known channel, and that every reset be logged with the verification evidence. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-05, FINDING-05 -->

To enact this control IT should update help desk procedures, maintain an auditable log of reset requests and outcomes, include supervisory review for resets that permit sensitive access and coordinate with HR to ensure employee contact records are current; the chief financial officer is accountable for the corporate rule and the IT lead is the practical owner, with implementation in the proposed second stage. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-05 -->

## A written route must exist for allegations about senior people to reach an independent recipient
Route for allegations against senior people is the priority control to ensure allegations involving senior people are handled independently, and the organisation should publish a documented route that takes such allegations to an independent recipient and ensure staff know how to use it. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-06 -->

The chief executive is accountable and the board or its audit committee is the practical owner for this route; implementation involves documenting the intake process, identifying the independent recipient, training staff and keeping evidence that people have been told how to raise concerns without fear of reprisal, with the work scheduled for the proposed second stage. 
<!-- refs: PRIORITY-06 -->

# Management should recognise that a small set of controls will materially reduce exposure and that decisions are needed quickly

## A focused programme to establish organisation-started verification routes and to settle open questions will change exposure
The evidence from the answers points to a coherent set of actions: settle the outstanding questions functions reported as unknown, implement organisation-started verification for bank and beneficiary changes, strengthen identity verification at hiring, require independent verification for customer detail changes and make help desk resets independent of caller-supplied contact details, because these changes directly interrupt the most credible pathways by which ordinary requests are turned into payments or account takeovers. 
<!-- refs: THEME-UNCERTAINTY, THEME-VERIFY, PRIORITY-01, PRIORITY-02, PRIORITY-03, PRIORITY-04, PRIORITY-05 -->

## Within the proposed first ninety days management should have clear owners for all open items and the priority controls scheduled for design and rollout
A practical immediate step is for the leadership team to commission the proposed first stage, ask the named owners to deliver the outstanding facts and design templates, and for the board or its audit or risk committee to expect the priority control designs in the proposed second stage so controls can be started and reported in the proposed third stage.
