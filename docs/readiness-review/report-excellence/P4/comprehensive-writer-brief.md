## Fraud pathways (approved structures)

Selection rule: Findings with an approved scenario structure, one per theme where possible; up to 4.

### PATHWAY-01: An email account is taken over and the organisation's own invoices are used to divert its income (from FINDING-04)

- Actor: An outsider who gains access to a staff mailbox through a stolen password.
- Opportunity: The mailbox has no multi-factor sign-in, so a password is enough to enter it, read correspondence and send from it.
- Entry point: A phishing message or a password reused from another site.
- Mechanism: The attacker watches correspondence with clients and, at the right moment, sends a genuine-looking invoice or notice with changed bank details from the real account.
- Weakness relied on: Email accounts are not protected by multi-factor sign-in.
- Concealment: The message comes from the organisation's real address, so clients have no reason to doubt it; the attacker may set rules that hide replies from the mailbox owner.
- Consequence: Client payments are lost, the organisation may still need to recover its fees, and clients' trust in its correspondence is damaged.
- Warning indicators: Clients querying a change of bank details; Unfamiliar sign-ins or forwarding rules on a mailbox; Sent messages the owner did not write; A drop in expected receipts
- Containment: Reset the account and remove any forwarding rules, notify clients through a known channel, and preserve the sign-in and message logs.
- Long-term response: Every email account is protected by multi-factor sign-in, and clients are told how the organisation will confirm any change of bank details.

### PATHWAY-02: A cloned voice of a senior person instructs an urgent payment (from FINDING-01)

- Actor: An outsider using a cloned voice, a voice note or a manipulated video of an executive or owner, built from recordings that are publicly available.
- Opportunity: Staff act on instructions from senior people, and recognising the voice has always been enough.
- Entry point: A call or voice note, apparently from the chief executive or owner, asking for an urgent and confidential payment.
- Mechanism: The instruction is urgent and comes from a voice the employee knows, so the payment is made outside the normal process or pushed through it quickly.
- Weakness relied on: There is no agreed method, such as a call-back on a known number or a code word, that verifies a request made by phone, voice note or video, and executives are not bound by it.
- Concealment: The employee believes the instruction was genuine and the payment appears properly authorised until the executive is asked about it.
- Consequence: A payment lost to an account that is quickly emptied, with the added harm that staff no longer trust instructions from their own leaders.
- Warning indicators: An instruction that insists on secrecy or bypasses the normal process; Urgency with a reason why the executive cannot be called back; A request from an unfamiliar number or account; A new beneficiary on an executive instruction
- Containment: Call the executive back on a known number, contact the bank to recall the payment, and preserve the call, message and payment records.
- Long-term response: An agreed verification method applies to every request made by phone, voice note or video, to everyone including executives, and staff are expected to use it.

### PATHWAY-03: A payment instruction received by email is acted on without separate confirmation (from FINDING-03)

- Actor: An outsider sending a convincing email that appears to come from the owner, a supplier or a client.
- Opportunity: Payment, salary and urgent requests received by email are processed on the strength of the email itself.
- Entry point: An email instructing an urgent payment or a change of bank details, often timed for when the sender is known to be unavailable.
- Mechanism: The request looks routine and comes from a familiar name, so it is processed in the ordinary way.
- Weakness relied on: There is no organisation-wide rule requiring every payment, salary or urgent request received by email to be confirmed through a separate, known channel.
- Concealment: The payment is properly processed against a request that appears genuine; the fraud is found only when the supposed sender is asked.
- Consequence: A payment lost to an account that is quickly emptied, and recovery that depends on how quickly the bank is alerted.
- Warning indicators: Urgency and a reason why the sender cannot be reached; A new beneficiary or changed bank details; A slightly different email address; An instruction to keep the request confidential
- Containment: Contact the bank to recall the payment, confirm with the supposed sender through a known number, and preserve the email and payment records.
- Long-term response: Every payment, salary or urgent request received by email is confirmed through a separate, known channel before it is acted on.

### PATHWAY-04: A help desk reset hands an attacker the account that moves money (from FINDING-05)

- Actor: An outsider impersonating an employee to the help desk or the IT provider.
- Opportunity: Password and multi-factor resets are made on the caller's word, and the systems that move money accept the reset account.
- Entry point: A call or message to the help desk asking for a password or multi-factor reset, often claiming a lost phone.
- Mechanism: The reset is made, the attacker signs in as the employee and uses the employee's access to change bank details or release payments.
- Weakness relied on: Help desk resets are not verified through a route the caller does not control, and systems that move money are not protected by strong sign-in.
- Concealment: The activity is carried out under a real employee's account, so it looks like ordinary work.
- Consequence: Payments or changes made under a legitimate account, and the difficulty of proving who acted.
- Warning indicators: A reset request claiming a lost phone or urgency; A reset followed quickly by payment or bank-detail activity; Sign-ins from an unusual location or device; An employee unaware of a reset made in their name
- Containment: Lock the account, reverse the changes, contact the bank about any payment, and preserve the help desk and sign-in records.
- Long-term response: Every password or multi-factor reset is verified through a route the caller does not control, and systems that move money require strong sign-in.

## Control blueprints

### BLUEPRINT-01: Verification for calls, voice notes and video

- Current: Some staff check unusual requests, depending on their own judgement.
- Target: An agreed method, such as a call-back on a known number or a code word, verifies any request made by phone, voice note or video; it applies to everyone, including executives, and staff know they are expected to use it.
- Objective: A cloned voice or video instruction is obeyed
- Accountable executive: Chief financial officer; process owner: Chief financial officer
- Population: Staff who act on requests by phone, voice note or video; frequency: each year
- Record: Verification method record: the agreed method, briefing records and the results of each test.
- Independent check: A periodic review by the fraud risk lead, who samples the records and reports exceptions.
- Escalation and failure response: Where the rule was not followed, establish why, brief the people concerned again and check whether any payment or disclosure resulted.
- Service level: Completed each year; exceptions answered before the next review.
- Effectiveness measure: All staff who act on requests by phone, voice note or video were briefed by the end of the period, and the most recent test found the rule followed in every case tested, including requests that appeared to come from executives.
- Connected controls: Pressure triggers verification; Changes to call-back details verified; Gift cards and PIN requests banned
- Activities: Write the rule and have it approved by the chief executive. | Brief staff who act on requests by phone, voice note or video, including executives and their assistants, and record who has been briefed. | Test the rule at least once a year, for example with a simulated request, and act on the result.

- Activities: Write the procedure, naming who decides and who acts at each step. | Brief the people with a role in it, and keep the contact details it relies on current. | Test it each year with an exercise or a real case, and record what changed as a result.

## Decisions

### DECISION-01: Name one accountable executive for each area with open or unresolved scenarios: digital channels (10).

- Why: Each scenario needs one person with authority over the process, the system and the people involved.
- Owner: Chief executive; when: Before design work starts.; if delayed: Open scenarios have no owner, and the controls that stop them stay unassigned.
- Option "One accountable executive per area": Each area with open scenarios has one executive accountable for its controls, as the method suggests. Effort: Low to decide; ongoing for each executive. Benefit: Clear ownership that matches how the organisation is run. Trade-off: Requirements that span areas, such as how changes to payment details are verified, need coordination across owners.
- Option "One executive for the whole programme": A single executive owns every open scenario and directs area leads. Effort: Concentrated on one person. Benefit: One point of accountability and a consistent approach. Trade-off: Area executives may treat the work as someone else's.
- Option "Area owners with a programme coordinator": Area executives own their scenarios, and the fraud risk lead coordinates requirements that span areas and reports progress. Effort: Moderate. Benefit: Ownership stays in the areas while cross-area requirements are handled once. Trade-off: Needs a coordinator with enough standing to hold owners to account.
- MK recommended route: Area owners with a programme coordinator. Rationale: The open scenarios sit in the operating areas, so ownership belongs there; several of the requirements span areas and are best designed once.

### DECISION-02: Decide the order in which the open scenarios are addressed, using explicit factors.

- Why: MK has not approved a method for rating fraud risks, so this report does not rank them, and neither its order nor the method's tiers is a ranking.
- Owner: Chief executive; when: Before design work starts.; if delayed: Effort is spread across every scenario at once, or follows the order of this report, which is not a ranking.
- Option "Explicit factors, settled facts first": Answers to settle come first; then each open scenario is weighed on how far its control is reported in place, whether money, pay, cash or stock can leave without it, how much of the activity exists, and what management already knows of incidents. Effort: Moderate: a structured management session. Benefit: A defensible order that management can explain to the oversight body. Trade-off: Depends on information management must supply, such as volumes and known incidents.
- Option "Area by area": One area is completed before the next begins. Effort: Low to plan. Benefit: Simple to manage and report. Trade-off: Requirements that span areas are designed several times, and exposure in later areas waits.
- Option "Cross-area requirements first": Requirements that span areas, such as verifying changes to payment details, are designed once and applied everywhere, before area-specific controls. Effort: Moderate. Benefit: One design closes several scenarios at once. Trade-off: Area-specific exposures wait until the shared designs are complete.
- MK recommended route: Explicit factors, settled facts first, applying cross-area requirements once where they arise. Rationale: An order of work should rest on facts management can state and defend to its oversight body, and designing shared requirements once avoids building the same control several times.

### DECISION-03: Have the owners receive the status of the open and unresolved scenarios at each of its meetings until each is closed, deferred with a reason or accepted.

- Why: Regular reporting keeps the scenarios visible to the people who can remove blockers.
- Owner: Chief executive; when: From the first meeting after this report.; if delayed: Progress is not visible until the next assessment.
- Option "At each meeting of the oversight body": A one-page status per open scenario at each meeting until it is closed, deferred with a reason or accepted. Effort: Low. Benefit: Keeps the scenarios visible to the people who can remove blockers. Trade-off: Adds to the oversight body's agenda.
- Option "Quarterly summary": A consolidated summary each quarter. Effort: Low. Benefit: Less frequent demand on the oversight body. Trade-off: Blockers may wait a quarter before they are seen.
- Option "Exceptions only": Only scenarios that are late or blocked are reported. Effort: Lowest. Benefit: Focuses attention on problems. Trade-off: The oversight body does not see progress, and a scenario can drift without being reported as an exception.
- MK recommended route: At each meeting of the oversight body, until each open scenario is closed, deferred with a reason or accepted. Rationale: Regular reporting is what keeps a programme moving once the first controls are in place.
