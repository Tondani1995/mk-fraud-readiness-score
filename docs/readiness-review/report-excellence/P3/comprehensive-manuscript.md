# The group has a working fraud framework at the centre, and the task now is to build controls into the operations it governs

On its own answers, the group has the core controls in place for [[N-REPORTED-HELD]] of the [[N-APPLY]] fraud scenarios that apply to it, and a core control reported as a gap in [[N-GAP]]. Those figures conceal a sharper pattern. In fraud governance, fraud risk assessment, finance and banking, and detection and response, every core control is reported in place. In third parties, people and payroll, customers, assets and stock, and digital channels, almost every core control is reported as a gap.
<!-- refs: N-REPORTED-HELD, N-APPLY, N-GAP, L-AREAS-HELD, L-AREAS-OPEN -->

MK's reading is that the group has built a fraud programme from the centre outward and has not yet carried it into the operations. Ownership of fraud risk is written into an executive's role, the risks are assessed, the finance and banking controls are described as working, and there are channels to report and investigate concerns. These are the parts of a programme that tell an organisation what its exposure is and how it will respond. The parts that prevent and detect fraud inside the processes, where suppliers are paid, staff are paid, customers receive credit and refunds, stock is held and instructions arrive, are largely absent.
<!-- refs: THEME-STRUCTURE, N-AREAS-HELD, N-AREAS-OPEN -->

This is a position with real strengths and a clear direction. The group does not need to rebuild its governance; it needs to give its operating executives ownership of the controls in their processes and to apply, in each of them, the same discipline the centre already applies. The scale of the open register, [[N-FINDINGS-REGISTER]] scenarios, should not obscure that much of it rests on a small number of shared requirements.
<!-- refs: THEME-STRUCTURE, N-FINDINGS-REGISTER -->

[[N-THEMES|W]] themes carry the diagnosis. The first is the gap between the framework and the operations. The second is a single weakness that recurs across [[THEME-VERIFY-NAREAS|w]] areas: changes to bank details, customer details and identity are accepted on the strength of the request itself, through contact details the requester supplied, a familiar voice or a document that looks right. The third is that no independent review examines procurement or payroll, the processes through which money leaves the organisation in volume and on a routine cycle.
<!-- refs: THEME-STRUCTURE, THEME-VERIFY, THEME-VERIFY-NAREAS, THEME-X-11 -->

The themes interact in a way that matters for the response. The verification weakness gives an outsider the opportunity to divert a payment or obtain credit; the absence of independent review means that nothing looking across the payments would notice; and because the controls sit in processes the centre does not run, the risk function's assessment can identify the exposure without being able to close it. A loss in these areas is therefore more likely to be found by a supplier, a customer or a whistle-blower than by a control designed to find it.
<!-- refs: THEME-VERIFY, THEME-X-11, THEME-STRUCTURE -->

The exposure is concrete. A supplier's bank details can be changed by an impostor and confirmed through the impostor's own number. A senior review of supplier payments has been agreed but does not run, so split orders and duplicate invoices pass unseen. Credit is granted on documents checked only when something looks wrong, stock is counted by the people who hold it, and payroll is reviewed only at the level of totals.
<!-- refs: FINDING-01, FINDING-02, FINDING-03, FINDING-06, FINDING-05 -->

Leadership's priorities follow. In the first thirty days, the chief executive should name an accountable executive for each operating area and decide the order of work on explicit factors, designing the verification requirement once for every area it affects. Over the following two months, the priority controls should be designed and put to work: confirmation of supplier bank changes through details on file, a call-back rule that holds whatever a caller appears to be, the monthly senior review of supplier payments, verification of credit applications with their source, and verification of customer detail changes away from sales. By twelve months, each should have met its success measure over a full cycle, procurement and payroll should have been examined independently, and the board should be receiving a status report on every open area.
<!-- refs: DECISION-01, DECISION-02, BLUEPRINT-01, BLUEPRINT-02, BLUEPRINT-03, BLUEPRINT-04, BLUEPRINT-05, PHASE-STABILISE, PHASE-ESTABLISH, PHASE-MATURE -->

Every question in the assessment was answered and no pair of answers conflicts, so the position is not limited by missing or inconsistent information.

# [[N-THEMES|W]] themes explain the position, and each one makes the others harder to see

The themes below draw the individual answers into a management view. The map shows where each appears; the discussion that follows explains how they interact.
<!-- refs: THEME-STRUCTURE, THEME-VERIFY, THEME-X-11 -->

[[exhibit:theme-map]]

## The framework at the centre has not been carried into the operating processes

The [[N-AREAS-HELD|w]] central areas are reported as complete and the [[N-AREAS-OPEN|w]] operating areas as almost entirely open. A framework of this kind establishes who owns fraud risk, what the exposure is and how concerns will be handled. It does not stop a supplier being paid into the wrong account, a refund being redirected or stock leaving a store; those outcomes depend on controls inside the processes.
<!-- refs: THEME-STRUCTURE, L-AREAS-HELD, L-AREAS-OPEN -->

The most likely explanation is that ownership of fraud controls has stayed with the risk and finance functions that built the framework, while the executives who run procurement, payroll, customers, stock and digital channels have not taken on the controls in their processes. The response is therefore organisational before it is technical: the operating executives must own the controls, with the risk function setting the standard and checking that it is met.
<!-- refs: THEME-STRUCTURE, DECISION-01 -->

## The same verification weakness recurs across suppliers, payroll, customers and digital channels

Across [[THEME-VERIFY-AREAS]], [[THEME-VERIFY-N]] practices that would verify a request through a route the requester does not control are reported weaker than in place. Supplier bank changes are confirmed through contact details in the request, a familiar voice is accepted, employee and customer bank and delivery changes are not consistently verified with the person concerned, credit documents are checked only on appearance, and requests by phone, voice note or video depend on individual judgement.
<!-- refs: THEME-VERIFY-AREAS, THEME-VERIFY-N, FINDING-01, FINDING-03, FINDING-04, FINDING-07 -->

Most payment diversion, impersonation and application fraud succeeds by supplying the confirmation a process asks for, rather than by defeating a control. The defence in every case is the same: verification through details the organisation already holds, by a channel the requester did not choose, applied to everyone including executives. Because the weakness is shared, so is the remedy, and designing it once is both cheaper and more reliable than solving it separately in each process.
<!-- refs: THEME-VERIFY, DECISION-02 -->

## No independent review examines procurement or payroll

Independent review of procurement and of payroll are both reported weaker than in place, across [[THEME-X-11-AREAS]]. In the same processes the senior review of supplier payments is agreed but does not run, and the payroll review looks only at totals.
<!-- refs: THEME-X-11-N, THEME-X-11-AREAS, FINDING-02, FINDING-05 -->

Independent review is the control that looks across transactions and asks whether the controls inside the process actually operate. Without it, fictitious suppliers, split orders, duplicate invoices and ghost employees can pass every individual check, and the new controls the group puts in place will have no one confirming that they work.
<!-- refs: THEME-X-11, PATHWAY-03 -->

The themes reinforce one another. The verification weakness provides the opportunity, the absence of independent review delays discovery, and the structural gap means the function that understands the exposure does not run the processes where it sits. The programme in this report addresses all three together: ownership first, a shared verification standard, and independent review of the processes that move the most money.
<!-- refs: THEME-STRUCTURE, THEME-VERIFY, THEME-X-11, PHASE-STABILISE -->

<!-- section: COMP-FINDINGS -->
# [[N-FINDINGS|W]] findings show how the themes appear in the processes that move money, goods and credit

The findings are the scenarios through which each theme appears most clearly: within each theme, the scenario with more core controls reported absent, or with the most related practices also reported weaker. They follow the method's order of areas, which is not a ranking; The full register is in the supporting workbook.
<!-- refs: N-FINDINGS, N-FINDINGS-REGISTER -->

## Supplier bank changes are confirmed through details the fraudster can supply

Changes to supplier bank details are confirmed, but sometimes using contact details taken from the request itself, and a follow-up call from someone who sounds like the supplier's usual contact is accepted as confirmation.
<!-- refs: FINDING-01 -->

Both answers describe a confirmation the fraudster controls. In the wider control system, the change feeds the supplier master file, from which every later payment is made, so a single accepted change diverts every payment until the supplier complains. The senior review of supplier payments, which would look for changed bank details, does not yet run, so nothing downstream catches it.
<!-- refs: FINDING-01, FINDING-02 -->

The target design separates the confirmation from the request entirely: details on file, a call-back that holds whatever the caller appears to be, and a second person who did not capture the change. It is the first blueprint in the target environment.
<!-- refs: FINDING-01, BLUEPRINT-01, BLUEPRINT-02 -->

[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-01]]

## The senior review of supplier payments has been agreed but does not run

The monthly senior review of supplier payments is set out but not working, monitoring of split orders is set out but not operating, reviewing payment data for unusual patterns is not in place, and independent review of procurement is only partly in place.
<!-- refs: FINDING-02 -->

These answers describe one missing capability, looking at payments as a population, which the group has evidently decided it needs. It is the detective control behind several preventive ones: it would catch a changed bank detail that slipped through, a duplicate invoice, a split purchase or a new supplier paid unusually often.
<!-- refs: FINDING-02, FINDING-01 -->

Because the design already exists, the target is operational: a named senior manager outside payments processing, a monthly cycle, and a first review over the last twelve months.
<!-- refs: FINDING-02, BLUEPRINT-03 -->

[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-02]]

## Credit is granted on documents checked only when something looks wrong

Supporting documents for credit applications are verified only if something looks wrong. Independent approval of credit, bureau and fraud database checks and the investigation of early defaults are each reported only partly in place.
<!-- refs: FINDING-03 -->

A check triggered by appearance is the check that generated payslips and bank statements are designed to pass. In the control system around it, the absence of early-default investigation means losses from fraudulent applications are likely to be booked as bad debt, so the scheme can repeat with the same templates.
<!-- refs: FINDING-03, PATHWAY-04 -->

The target verifies the key information in every application with its source before approval, and treats a first-instalment default as a prompt to look for application fraud.
<!-- refs: FINDING-03, BLUEPRINT-04 -->

[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-03]]

## Customer refund and delivery details can be changed by sales staff

Most changes to customer details are verified with the customer, but sales staff can still make them. Defined onboarding is not in place, business customers are only partly verified, and details shared between accounts are not flagged.
<!-- refs: FINDING-04 -->

Customer details are where refunds and deliveries are directed, so the ability to change them is the ability to redirect money and goods. Allowing the people who serve customers to make the change removes the separation that would require a second person to be involved, and without flags on shared details, several accounts paying into one bank account would not stand out.
<!-- refs: FINDING-04, THEME-VERIFY -->

The exposure is regulatory as well as financial. An impostor who changes a customer's details usually gains access to that customer's personal information, which POPIA treats as a security compromise that section 22 requires the group to report to the Information Regulator and the customer. The target moves the change out of sales and verifies it with the customer through details already on file.
<!-- refs: FINDING-04, BLUEPRINT-05, LAW-01 -->

[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-04]]

## Payroll is reviewed only at the level of totals

Each payroll run is looked at by someone other than the preparer, but only at the totals. Headcount verification is not in place, payroll data analysis is set out but not operating, and independent review of payroll is only partly in place.
<!-- refs: FINDING-05 -->

A ghost employee, or a leaver left on the payroll, does not move the totals enough to be noticed, and without headcount verification or data analysis there is no other point at which the payroll is compared with the people actually employed. Because changes to employees' bank details are also not consistently verified with the employee, the same payroll can be diverted as well as inflated.
<!-- refs: FINDING-05, THEME-VERIFY -->

The target is a detailed review of each run before payment by someone who did not prepare it, covering starters, leavers and changes, with variances explained. It is among the first responses and is specified in full in the workbook.
<!-- refs: FINDING-05, PRIORITY-06 -->

[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-05]]

## Stock is counted by the people who hold it

The stores team counts the stock and someone else glances at the results. How stock is recorded, full counts, explaining differences before adjusting, approving write-offs and tracking shrinkage are all reported weaker than in place.
<!-- refs: FINDING-06 -->

These practices form one chain: a reliable record of what should be there, a count by someone independent of custody, an explanation for every difference before the record is changed, and a view of losses by location. Each link is reported weak, so a loss can be counted away, written off and never examined.
<!-- refs: FINDING-06 -->

The target is independent blind counts with every difference explained before adjustment and shrinkage tracked by store, so that a location where losses concentrate becomes visible.
<!-- refs: FINDING-06, PATHWAY-01 -->

[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-06]]

## Instructions by phone, voice note or video depend on individual judgement

Whether an unusual instruction received by phone, voice note or video is checked depends on the judgement of the person who receives it. The supporting practices are partial too: changes to call-back details are verified only in part, and executives and their teams are only partly prepared for impersonation.
<!-- refs: FINDING-07 -->

Recognition no longer confirms identity, because voices and faces can be cloned from public recordings. A check left to judgement is skipped under pressure, and an impersonated executive applies exactly that pressure. The weakness connects directly with supplier bank changes, where a familiar voice is already accepted.
<!-- refs: FINDING-07, FINDING-01, THEME-VERIFY -->

The target is an agreed verification method that applies to everyone, including executives, and that staff are expected to use whatever the caller appears to be.
<!-- refs: FINDING-07, BLUEPRINT-02 -->

[[exhibit:finding:FINDING-07]]

<!-- section: COMP-PATHWAYS -->
# The open controls create [[N-PATHWAYS|w]] practised routes to loss, each hard to see from inside the process

In each pathway below, one weakness provides the opportunity and another delays its discovery. They are plausible sequences built from the answers and MK's scenario library, and they do not suggest that any of these events has occurred.
<!-- refs: PATHWAY-01, PATHWAY-02, PATHWAY-03, PATHWAY-04 -->

## Stock leaves a store, and the count is made to agree

An employee with custody of stock, sometimes with a driver, a supplier or a colleague, removes stock over time. Because the stores team counts its own stock, the counter records the expected quantities or attributes the shortfall to damage or error.
<!-- refs: PATHWAY-01, FINDING-06 -->

Differences are not explained before adjustment and shrinkage is not tracked by location, so nothing marks the store out. The loss is absorbed into margin, and the write-offs that conceal it look like the ordinary cost of retail.
<!-- refs: PATHWAY-01 -->

[[exhibit:pathway:PATHWAY-01]]

## A supplier's bank details are changed and the next payment run pays the fraudster

An outsider who has compromised a supplier's email requests a change of bank details, on the supplier's letterhead and with a contact number. When finance calls that number, the fraudster confirms the change; sometimes a follow-up call in the usual contact's voice is enough.
<!-- refs: PATHWAY-02, FINDING-01 -->

The next genuine invoice is approved and paid to the new account in the normal run. Every document is genuine, and with no monthly review of supplier payments looking for changed bank details, nothing looks wrong until the real supplier asks why it has not been paid.
<!-- refs: PATHWAY-02, FINDING-02 -->

[[exhibit:pathway:PATHWAY-02]]

## A purchase is split below the approval limit, and nobody looks across the orders

A buyer splits a purchase into orders that each fall below the approval limit, or a supplier resubmits an invoice already paid. Each transaction passes its own checks.
<!-- refs: PATHWAY-03, FINDING-02 -->

The pattern exists only in the payment data, and because the senior review has not started and split orders are not monitored, nobody examines it. Purchases are made without competition and duplicate payments go unrecovered until a supplier, an auditor or a whistle-blower raises it.
<!-- refs: PATHWAY-03 -->

[[exhibit:pathway:PATHWAY-03]]

## Fabricated payslips secure credit that is never repaid

An applicant, sometimes working with others, applies for credit with a payslip and bank statement generated to show income they do not have. The documents look genuine, so the check that is triggered only by appearance is never triggered.
<!-- refs: PATHWAY-04, FINDING-03 -->

Credit is approved, goods are taken and the account defaults. Because early defaults are not consistently investigated, the loss is booked as bad debt, and the same templates can be used again before anyone recognises the pattern.
<!-- refs: PATHWAY-04, FINDING-03 -->

[[exhibit:pathway:PATHWAY-04]]

<!-- section: COMP-TARGET -->
# The target environment verifies every change through a route the requester does not control, and looks across payments as well as at them

The blueprints below cover the priority controls. They share a principle: a change that redirects money, goods or credit is confirmed through details the group already holds, and the payments that result are reviewed as a whole by someone outside the process. Each explains the current position, how the control should operate and how management will know it works; the panel summarises its operating design. The complete specification for every control in the register, [[N-CONTROLS]] in all, is in the supporting workbook.
<!-- refs: N-CONTROLS, BLUEPRINT-01, BLUEPRINT-03 -->

## Supplier bank changes confirmed through details already on file

Changes are confirmed today, but sometimes using contact details in the request. In the target state, every change is confirmed with the supplier through contact details already on file before it takes effect, by someone other than the person who captured it, and each change is logged with how and by whom it was confirmed.
<!-- refs: BLUEPRINT-01 -->

The control works with the call-back rule below and with the monthly payment review, which looks for changed bank details as one of its patterns. Its measure is simple to read: every change in the period confirmed before it took effect, or a recorded and approved exception.
<!-- refs: BLUEPRINT-01, BLUEPRINT-02, BLUEPRINT-03 -->

[[exhibit:blueprint:BLUEPRINT-01]]

## A call-back rule that holds whatever the caller appears to be

A follow-up call from the supplier is accepted as confirmation today. In the target state, a call back on the number on file is required for every change request, whatever an incoming call or video appears to show, and staff are briefed on why and tested on it.
<!-- refs: BLUEPRINT-02 -->

The rule is the operating form of the verification theme. Once designed for supplier changes, the same rule can be applied to employee bank changes, customer details and instructions from executives, which is why it is best owned once and applied across areas.
<!-- refs: BLUEPRINT-02, THEME-VERIFY -->

[[exhibit:blueprint:BLUEPRINT-02]]

## A monthly senior review of supplier payments that actually runs

The review is agreed but not working. In the target state, a senior manager who does not process payments reviews each month's supplier payments for unfamiliar suppliers, unusual amounts, duplicates and changed bank details, and follows up each query to a recorded answer.
<!-- refs: BLUEPRINT-03 -->

The review is the detective layer behind the preventive controls on bank changes, and it connects with monitoring of split orders and independent review of procurement. Its first run should cover the previous twelve months.
<!-- refs: BLUEPRINT-03, THEME-X-11 -->

[[exhibit:blueprint:BLUEPRINT-03]]

## Credit applications verified with their source before approval

Documents are checked today only when something looks wrong. In the target state, the key information in every credit application is verified with its source, such as the employer, the bank or a verification service, before credit is approved, and the application file records what was verified and how.
<!-- refs: BLUEPRINT-04 -->

The control connects with independent approval of credit, bureau and fraud database checks and the investigation of early defaults, which together turn application fraud from an unrecognised bad debt into a visible pattern.
<!-- refs: BLUEPRINT-04 -->

[[exhibit:blueprint:BLUEPRINT-04]]

## Customer detail changes verified and made outside sales

Most changes are verified today, but sales staff can still make them. In the target state, every change to a customer's refund account, delivery address or contact numbers is verified with the customer through details on file and made by someone outside sales.
<!-- refs: BLUEPRINT-05 -->

Separating the change from the sale restores the second person whose involvement deters collusion, and flagging details shared between accounts would show where several customers are paid into one account.
<!-- refs: BLUEPRINT-05, FINDING-04 -->

[[exhibit:blueprint:BLUEPRINT-05]]

<!-- section: COMP-OPMODEL -->
# Ownership must move from the centre into the operating areas, with the centre setting the standard and checking it

The group reports that executive accountability for fraud risk is written into a named role and that the board holds final oversight. The target operating model keeps both and adds what the themes show is missing: owners for the controls inside the operations. The chief executive remains accountable overall; the fraud risk lead sets the standards, challenges the owners and consolidates reporting; the owners of finance, customers, payroll and the other operating areas run the controls in their processes and keep the records; and the investigations lead handles reports and alerts independently.
<!-- refs: ROLE-OVERSIGHT, ROLE-EXECUTIVE, ROLE-RISK, ROLE-OWNERS, ROLE-DETECT -->

[[exhibit:operating-model]]

Status moves upward each month from the area owners through the fraud risk lead to the chief executive, and to the board at each meeting. The independent check sits outside this line: someone who does not run procurement or payroll examines those processes and reports to the chief executive and the board.
<!-- refs: ROLE-RISK, ROLE-OVERSIGHT, THEME-X-11, DECISION-03 -->

The management information that makes this work is small and specific. Each month the fraud risk lead should consolidate, for the chief executive, the stage reached by every open scenario, each control measure missed or escalated in the period, and the outcome of each report or alert the investigations lead has closed. The board needs less detail and a clearer signal: whether each operating area now has an owner, whether the priority controls have completed a full operating cycle, and which escalations called for an executive decision. The scorecard that follows is designed to produce exactly that picture.

<!-- section: COMP-CONCLUSION -->
# The group has built the hardest part of a fraud programme, and the next year should carry it into the operations

The group should recognise that it has done what many organisations of its size have not: fraud risk is owned, assessed and governed, and there is a capability to respond. What it has not yet done is put controls into the operations where fraud would occur, and the open register is the measure of that gap.
<!-- refs: THEME-STRUCTURE, N-FINDINGS-REGISTER -->

The transition required is organisational before it is technical. Operating executives must own the controls in their processes; a single verification standard should protect every change that redirects money, goods or credit; and someone independent must look across procurement and payroll. Those foundations make the remaining work tractable.
<!-- refs: DECISION-01, THEME-VERIFY, THEME-X-11 -->

By ninety days, every open area should have an owner, the order of work should be set down, and the priority controls should be running their first cycles. By twelve months, they should have met their measures over a full cycle, the remaining scenarios should be in progress in the order management decided, and the board should be receiving status on every area.
