Supplier payments are diverted through changed bank details
SUPPLIER PAYMENT DIVERSION
 A fraudster, often impersonating the supplier by email, voice or video, has the bank account changed. Changed supplier bank details are the most common route for payment fraud, and cloned voices now defeat call-backs that rely on recognition. What the answers show: A follow-up call from the 'supplier' is accepted as confirmation. Changes are confirmed, but sometimes using contact details taken from the request itself. The procedure requires a call back on the number on file regardless of any incoming call or video, and staff know why. Every change is confirmed with the supplier using contact details already on file before it takes effect.
  • Payment redirection through false bank details or impersonation

PAYROLL MANIPULATION
 A request that appears to come from an employee changes where their salary is paid. Salary redirection through fake bank change requests is common and fast. What the answers show: Bank detail changes are made as requested. Every change is confirmed through a channel the organisation starts, such as a call to a number on file or in person, before it takes effect.

  • Ghost or fictitious employees and payees
  • Unauthorised pay increases, allowances or deductions

A payment instruction received by email is acted on
IDENTITY IMPERSONATION
 Changed details or urgent payments are processed on the strength of an email. Acting on payment instructions received by email is how business email compromise succeeds. What the answers show: Payment changes received by email are acted on as received. An organisation-wide rule requires confirmation through a separate, known channel for every payment, salary or urgent request received by email, and staff follow it.
  • Business email compromise and payment redirection
  • Takeover of staff accounts

An email account is taken over
IDENTITY IMPERSONATION
 A mailbox without strong sign-in is used to send or intercept payment instructions. Most business email compromise starts with an account that has no multi-factor authentication. What the answers show: Email accounts are protected by passwords only. Multi-factor authentication is required for every email account, including executives' and shared mailboxes, with no exceptions.
  • Takeover of staff accounts
  • Business email compromise and payment redirection
  • Spoofed or lookalike email in the organisation's name
  • Criminals impersonating the organisation to customers, suppliers and the public

A fictitious supplier is created and paid
SUPPLIER PAYMENT DIVERSION
 An insider or outsider adds a supplier that does not exist and invoices it. Fictitious suppliers enter through onboarding that can be skipped. What the answers show: Checks happen only when someone thinks to do them. Directors compared with staff: not in place. Dormant suppliers deactivated: not in place. Every new supplier goes through a defined process, with set checks and an approval, before it can be paid. Directors compared with staff: the answer to "Do you compare supplier directors, owners, addresses or bank accounts with your employee records?" is yes, across everything it should cover. Dormant suppliers deactivated: the answer to "Are suppliers that have not been used for a set period deactivated?" is yes, across everything it should cover. Suppliers re-checked: the answer to "Are existing suppliers re-checked periodically, not only when they are first added?" is yes, across everything it should cover. Directors and owners, including those behind holding companies, are identified for every new supplier from an independent source.
  • Fictitious or shell suppliers
  • Undisclosed conflicts of interest
  • Misrepresented supplier credentials
  • Duplicate or false invoices

Buyers take kickbacks
THIRD PARTY COLLUSION
 A long-standing buyer-supplier relationship hides favours. What the answers show: Buying staff do not declare interests. Everyone involved in buying declares interests each year and when circumstances change, and declarations are kept on record.
  • Undisclosed conflicts of interest
  • Kickbacks and bribery
  • Bid rigging and collusion
 RISK-FS-D4-09, CI-FS-D4-09
Refunds are stolen
DETECTION EVASION
 One person raises and approves refunds or credit notes to themselves or accomplices. Refunds processed and approved by one person are a classic way to steal. What the answers show: Approval is sought only for large refunds or when staff are unsure. Refunds are paid wherever the customer asks. Returns received before credit: not in place. Every refund and credit note is approved by someone other than the person who processes it. Every refund goes to the original payment method or to a customer account verified before payment. Returns received before credit: the answer to "Is every return matched to the goods physically coming back into stock before a credit is issued?" is yes, across everything it should cover. Claim evidence checked for AI: the answer to "Are photographs and documents supporting refund or return requests checked for manipulation, such as AI-generated images of damage?" is yes, across everything it should cover. Refund patterns reviewed: the answer to "Are refunds and credit notes reviewed for patterns, such as the same employee or customer, or amounts just below approval limits?" is yes, across everything it should cover.
  • False refunds, credit notes and returns
  • Stolen or diverted customer payments, including lapping
  • Fictitious sales and commission fraud
 RISK-FS-D7-01, CI-FS-D7-01
Credit is obtained with fabricated documents
DETECTION EVASION
 Fake payslips and bank statements, now easy to generate, secure credit that is never repaid. Application fraud with fabricated documents drives credit losses. What the answers show: Documents are checked only if something looks wrong. Customer payments are not matched to invoices. Statements sent from the system: not in place. The key information in every application is verified with the source, such as the employer, the bank or a verification service, before credit is approved. Every payment is matched to the right invoice, and unallocated receipts and misallocations are investigated and cleared promptly. Statements sent from the system: the answer to "Are customer statements sent directly from the system, so that an employee cannot hide a diverted payment from the customer?" is yes, across everything it should cover. Limit and terms changes approved: the answer to "Are changes to credit limits and payment terms approved and logged?" is yes, across everything it should cover. Every credit limit is approved by someone other than the salesperson, within set authority levels.
  • Application and credit fraud using forged documents
  • Unauthorised discounts, price overrides and side agreements
  • Customers using false or stolen identities
  • Stolen or diverted customer payments, including lapping

Fuel and fleet are misused
STOCK ASSET MISUSE
 Fuel cards or vehicles are used privately or fraudulently. What the answers show: Fuel purchases are not compared with vehicle use. Every vehicle's fuel purchases are compared with tracking data, distance travelled and expected consumption, and exceptions are investigated.
  • Fuel card fraud and vehicle misuse
  • Short deliveries and theft in transit
  • Fake proof of payment, deposit or delivery
  • Inflated maintenance and parts fraud
  • Organised theft of high-value materials

A cloned voice or video instruction is obeyed
IDENTITY IMPERSONATION
 A caller who sounds or looks like a known person is believed. Voices and faces can be cloned, so only an agreed verification method holds. What the answers show: Some staff check unusual requests, depending on their own judgement. Most changes are verified through the old details or another trusted route. Most executives with a public profile have agreed a verification method with their assistants and finance teams. An agreed method, such as a call-back on a known number or a code word, applies to everyone, including executives, and staff know they are expected to use it. Every change to a phone number or email used for verification is confirmed through the previous details or another trusted route before it is used. Every executive with a public voice or face has been briefed and has an agreed verification method that their assistants and finance teams use.
  • Deepfake voice and video impersonation of executives and staff
  • Business email compromise and payment redirection
  • Takeover of staff accounts
  • SIM swaps and intercepted one-time PINs
