Section 4 · Fraud scenario portfolio

How the schemes would work here

Plausible pathways for an organisation with the controls it reported, using the method's own description of each scheme. They are not allegations that any event has occurred.

A fictitious supplier is created and paid

S01 · procurement · tier 1
How it starts
An insider or outsider adds a supplier that does not exist and invoices it.
Why it matters
Fictitious suppliers enter through onboarding that can be skipped.
What the answers show
New supplier process: checks happen only when someone thinks to do them. Behind it, directors identified is partly in place, directors compared with staff is not in place and suppliers re-checked is set out, not yet operating.
What would interrupt it
Every new supplier goes through a defined process, with set checks and an approval, before it can be paid.
Warning signs
Fictitious or shell suppliers
Links
F01, FR01, C01, C02, C03, C04, C05

Supplier payments are diverted through changed bank details

S02 · procurement · tier 1
How it starts
A fraudster, often impersonating the supplier by email, voice or video, has the bank account changed.
Why it matters
Changed supplier bank details are the most common route for payment fraud, and cloned voices now defeat call-backs that rely on recognition.
What the answers show
Change confirmed with supplier: changes are confirmed, but sometimes using contact details taken from the request itself. Familiar caller still verified: a follow-up call from the 'supplier' is accepted as confirmation.
What would interrupt it
Every change is confirmed with the supplier using contact details already on file before it takes effect. The procedure requires a call back on the number on file regardless of any incoming call or video, and staff know why.
Warning signs
Payment redirection through false bank details or impersonation
Links
F02, FR02, C06, C07

A supplier is set up with an account it does not own

S03 · procurement · tier 1
How it starts
Payments go to an employee's or fraudster's account from the first invoice.
Why it matters
Confirming account ownership at onboarding stops both fictitious and impersonated suppliers being paid into accounts they do not own; added after the review's isolated-weakness probe.
What the answers show
Bank account verified: accounts are verified through a bank or third-party service for most new suppliers.
What would interrupt it
Every new supplier's account is verified through a bank or third-party service before the first payment.
Warning signs
Payment redirection through false bank details or impersonation; Fictitious or shell suppliers
Links
F03, FR03, C08

One person can order and approve payment

S04 · procurement · tier 1
How it starts
A buyer invents or inflates a purchase and approves its payment.
Why it matters
One person who can both order and approve payment can pay a supplier they invented.
What the answers show
Orderer is not payment approver: the same person can order and approve payment.
What would interrupt it
The duties are always separated, or any exception is approved and recorded.
Warning signs
Kickbacks and bribery; Duplicate or false invoices
Links
F04, FR04, C09

Invoices are paid for goods or services not received or not agreed

S05 · procurement · tier 1
How it starts
Overbilling, fictitious deliveries or inflated rates are paid.
Why it matters
Invoices paid without checking approval and receipt are how overbilling and fictitious deliveries get paid.
What the answers show
Three-way match: invoices are paid without being checked against what was approved or received. Behind it, approved order before commitment is partly in place, receipt recorded before payment is partly in place and quantities checked is not in place.
What would interrupt it
Every invoice is checked against an approved order, quote or contract and against confirmation of receipt before payment, and differences are resolved first.
Warning signs
Duplicate or false invoices; Goods or services paid for but not received
Links
F05, FR05, C14, C10, C11, C12, C13

An outsourced provider misuses its access

S06 · procurement · tier 1
How it starts
A provider that handles money, customers or data has wider access than the service needs.
Why it matters
Provider access wider than the service needs is how outsourced processes are abused; now applies wherever processes are outsourced, not only where nothing is bought.
What the answers show
Provider access limited: access is limited to what the service needs for the main providers.
What would interrupt it
Every provider's access is limited to what the service needs, logged and reviewed at least once a year.
Warning signs
Fraud inside outsourced service providers
Links
F06, FR06, C15
